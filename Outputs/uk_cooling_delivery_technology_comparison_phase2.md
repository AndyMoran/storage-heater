# UK Cooling Delivery Technologies: Comparing Architectures Against the Ground Truth (Phase 2)

**Prepared by:** Andy Moran, Heaviside Analytics
**Date:** September 2026
**Status:** Phase 2 of 2. Builds directly on the frozen Phase 1 document (*UK Cooling Demand and Grid Peak Load: Ground Truth*), which established that the UK's cooling requirement is a real, growing, climate-driven risk, while the resulting electricity demand and its coincidence with system peak load remain poorly quantified in official UK modelling. This phase asks which cooling-delivery architecture is best positioned against that ground truth — and, as set out below, finds that the single biggest gap identified in Phase 1 (nobody models coincident peak demand) survives entirely intact even in DESNZ's own dedicated 2026 study of this exact technology. Updated once since first drafted, to connect Phase 1's subsequent mortality/predictability findings (Section 7) to this phase's technology comparison.

Confidence tags as before: **confirmed directly** (read from the primary source itself — in this document, several key figures were independently re-verified by fetching the primary PDF a second time, not just taken from the research pass), **triangulated** (consistent but not independently primary-verified), or **not verified / no credible source found**.

## 1. Reframing the comparison: architecture, not category

As flagged in Phase 1's red-team review, "AC vs heat pump" is a false dichotomy — a reversible split air conditioner is, technically, an air-to-air heat pump. The distinction that actually matters is system architecture and incremental electrical load:

- **(a) Existing heating stock + separately-added cooling** — a gas boiler or air-to-water heat pump for space heating, with a standalone/portable/split AC unit bolted on separately for cooling.
- **(b) Integrated reversible air-to-air heat pump (RAAHP)** — one refrigerant-cycle system providing both heating and cooling.
- **(c) Air-to-water heat pump with cooling added on** — the UK's dominant installed heat pump type, with cooling capability retrofitted via fan coil units or cooling-mode underfloor heating.
- **(d) Peltier/thermoelectric reversible system** (e.g. TCS's Hummingbird) — heating and cooling delivered from the same solid-state hardware as an inherent property of the Peltier effect, not an add-on.

## 2. The DESNZ RAAHP report — the primary evidence base for architecture (b)

**DESNZ, *Understanding the suitability of reversible air-to-air heat pumps for domestic use in the UK*, published 9 July 2026** (59pp) is the most directly relevant primary source Phase 2 has, and several of its key findings were independently re-verified against the PDF itself in this session, not just extracted via a research pass.

**Methodology, confirmed:** DESNZ's own "Home Energy Dynamics" building-physics tool, modelling five archetypes (a post-2026 Future Homes Standard 3-bed detached; a post-2002 studio flat; a 1919–1944 3-bed semi-detached; a 1919–1944 4-bed detached; a pre-1919 2-bed mid-terrace), using Birmingham weather data only — both a typical year (TRY) and a short intense summer heatwave scenario (DSY2). **Worth flagging as a limitation for Phase 2's purposes:** a single-location model doesn't capture London or South East England's urban heat island effect, where both cooling demand and grid stress from summer heat are plausibly higher than Birmingham.

**Heating performance:** RAAHP-plus-supplementary-electric-heating delivered a useful-heat-to-energy-in ratio of roughly 2.5:1 to 4:1, against 3:1–3.5:1 for air-to-water heat pumps and ~0.9:1 for gas boilers. One archetype (the 1919–1944 4-bed detached) couldn't always hold the living room to target temperature in the coldest week — a genuine adequacy limitation, not a marketing gloss.

**Cooling performance:** with RAAHP cooling active, living-room temperature exceeded 25°C for no more than ~15% of the cooling season across archetypes. Older, smaller dwellings had minimal cooling need (under 1% of time above 25°C under typical weather); the new-build Future Homes Standard detached needed cooling far more — 8% of time above 25°C under typical weather, rising to 15% under the heatwave scenario. **This is a genuinely important finding for Phase 1's climate-driven-demand thesis: the best-insulated, newest UK housing stock is the one most prone to overheating and most reliant on active cooling** — an unintended consequence of fabric efficiency measures aimed at reducing heating demand.

**Running costs, confirmed directly** (re-fetched and verified against the primary PDF's Table 4, using the April–June 2026 Ofgem price cap: electricity 24.67p/kWh, gas 5.74p/kWh): for the studio flat archetype, RAAHP with a heat-pump hot-water tank came to £1,016/year including cooling — cheaper than the gas boiler baseline at £1,039/year, and cheaper than RAAHP-with-immersion-heater at £1,189/year. The report states directly: "the scenario with the RAAHP providing hot water is approximately 5% cheaper than the gas boiler scenario over the course of the year." For larger, older stock the pattern reverses: in the 1919–1944 semi-detached, RAAHP came to roughly £2,875/year against £2,267 for a gas boiler and £2,331 for an air-to-water heat pump — around 25% more expensive, because RAAHP's supplementary electric heating (bathroom electric radiators, immersion water heating) drags down blended efficiency in larger, colder-running homes. **Capital/installed cost was explicitly left to future work by DESNZ** — this report gives no installed-cost comparison at all, a real gap for anyone trying to do a full economic comparison.

**No cooling efficiency figure anywhere — confirmed directly**, re-checked against the primary PDF a second time. The report gives cooling energy demand in kWh and cost in £, but never states a cooling EER or cooling CoP for the RAAHP system. Peak cooling demand is likewise given only as a daily energy figure (roughly 25 kWh/day useful cooling in the Future Homes Standard dwelling under hot conditions) — not an instantaneous kW figure, and not a coincident system-peak figure.

**Suitability conclusions:** RAAHP performs best, on the report's own figures, in studio flats/small dwellings and comparably to an air-to-water heat pump in mid-terrace housing; it performs worst in larger detached and semi-detached homes, particularly 1919–1944 stock. **The report does not address listed buildings, absence of outdoor space, or planning/permitted-development constraints at all** — confirmed via an explicit check for these terms, none of which appear in the document. This creates a real, citable tension worth naming plainly: DESNZ's own report finds RAAHP's best economic case is the studio flat, but never asks whether that flat has anywhere to put the required outdoor unit — the same siting constraint (Phase 1's companion TCS document, and the property-gap analysis there) that rules conventional heat pumps out of much of the UK's flat and listed-building stock in the first place. DESNZ's report doesn't contradict that constraint; it simply never engages with it.

**Grid impact — confirmed directly, re-checked against the primary PDF a second time, and this is the single most important finding in this phase.** The report contains no mention of grid peak demand, coincident demand, or system-level electricity impacts anywhere. It assumes a flat-rate tariff throughout (not time-of-use), notes that a different tariff assumption "would not impact total energy demand, but could influence timing of energy demand and impact overall bills," and explicitly punts both variable-tariff analysis and any system-level question to future work. DESNZ's own report states its scope as "modelling the performance of RAAHPs across different conditions and archetypes, and compared to other heating systems, rather than archetype performance more broadly." **In plain terms: the UK government's own dedicated 2026 study of exactly this technology does not model whether or how its electricity demand coincides with system peak — confirming, one document further into 2026, precisely the gap Phase 1 identified in NESO's FES 2024 work.** This is now demonstrated twice, independently, across two different government bodies' 2026 evidence base.

## 3. Architecture (a): existing stock + bolted-on standalone AC

This is where the evidence thins out fastest, and it's worth being honest about that rather than filling the gap with plausible-sounding blog figures.

**No credible aggregated UK primary-source EER or electrical-demand (kW) figure for domestic AC units could be found — not verified.** MCS does not appear to certify or publish EER data for standalone air conditioning (its certification scope is oriented to renewable heating installers, not general AC). The Energy Saving Trust gives only a running-cost figure for a portable unit (£4.62–£5 per 24 hours in Great Britain, more in Northern Ireland) — confirmed directly from their own guidance — but no EER or kW figure, so it doesn't answer the efficiency-comparison question. There is a genuine UK-retained regulatory instrument that should have this — Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) No 626/2011, setting SEER-based energy labelling classes for household air conditioners, retained in UK law — but its text could not be fetched in this pass (blocked by the source's own access rules); this is a specific, named follow-up lead rather than a dead end. Every other source found on this question was retailer or content-farm material and has been excluded rather than cited, consistent with this project's standing rule against treating unverifiable commercial content as data.

**What can be said with confidence:** this architecture adds a second, fully separate appliance and its own separate electrical connection to whatever heating system is already installed — the incremental electrical load of the AC unit is additive to the existing heating system's load, with no shared hardware between the two. Beyond that structural point, the efficiency and typical demand of the bolted-on unit itself is an evidence gap, not a settled question.

## 4. Architecture (c): air-to-water heat pump with cooling added on

**No MCS or Heat Pump Association data could be found on how common this retrofit is in UK installations, its added cost, or its performance — not verified.** Searches surfaced only installer-marketing blog content, none from a credible primary body. This is consistent with Phase 1's recurring finding that UK domestic cooling data is thin across the board, and it should be treated as a genuine gap rather than assumed to be rare or uncommon without evidence either way.

## 5. Architecture (d): Peltier/thermoelectric (TCS Hummingbird)

Covered in depth in the companion TCS analysis and not re-verified here in full, but two points connect directly to this phase's findings. First, TCS's heating CoP (~4.9, DESNZ-funding-document-confirmed) has no published cooling-mode equivalent either — the same data gap DESNZ's own RAAHP report has for its own technology. On this specific point, TCS is not obviously behind the government's own preferred reversible technology; neither has published a cooling efficiency figure. Second, TCS's structural advantage — no outdoor unit — is precisely the constraint DESNZ's RAAHP report never engages with, and precisely the constraint that makes RAAHP's best economic use-case (flats) also its most siting-constrained one. If that tension is real, it's a genuine point in Peltier's favour for exactly the property segment DESNZ's own report says the reversible-heat-pump economics favour most.

## 6. A technical question that remains genuinely open

Whether an integrated reversible system (RAAHP, or the Peltier architecture) reduces total connected/peak electrical load compared with two separate appliances (a boiler or ASHP plus a separate AC unit) is an intuitively plausible claim — one compressor or one set of hardware instead of two — but **no UK-specific engineering or technical source was found that actually establishes this**, confirmed and not verified. A CIBSE Journal CPD module on hybrid heat pump and independent room unit integration was checked directly and discusses efficiency and lifecycle cost, but not connected load, diversity factors, or peak-demand comparison. This should be treated as an open technical question, not assumed true by plausibility — and it's arguably the single most useful question a dedicated engineering consultation (CIBSE, an MEP consultancy, or a manufacturer's own technical department) could resolve for a future phase.

## 7. Two findings from Phase 1's mortality/predictability update, and why they belong here

Phase 1 was subsequently updated with a section on temperature-related mortality and forecast predictability. Neither changes any figure or conclusion in this document, but two points from that update sharpen arguments already made here, so they're recorded against this phase rather than left stranded in Phase 1.

**Acute heat mortality tracks the demand event itself, unlike cold.** UKHSA's own reporting (confirmed directly in Phase 1, Section 6) shows cold-associated mortality is lagged — peaking around five days after a cold spell begins and remaining elevated for up to nine — while heat mortality tracks much more closely with the heat period itself. That reinforces this document's own framing (Section 1): the metric that matters for evaluating any of the four cooling architectures is coincident electrical load during system-stress windows, not average or annual demand, precisely because the health risk driving people to switch cooling on is itself concentrated in the same short window as the heat event and the associated grid stress documented in Phase 1's June 2026 example.

**Cold-wave forecast skill exceeding heat-wave forecast skill is a genuinely new angle for this phase, not just Phase 1 context.** Lavaysse et al. (*Climate Dynamics*, 2019, confirmed directly in Phase 1) find European cold waves forecastable up to 21 days against 15 days for heat waves in an ECMWF ensemble hindcast, with skill for both dropping sharply within the first week. If heat events are harder to forecast several days out than cold events, that has a direct bearing on which of the four architectures here is best positioned operationally, not just economically: a system that can be pre-cooled, scheduled, or otherwise operated flexibly ahead of a forecast heat event has less room to exploit that flexibility if the forecast itself is less reliable than the equivalent winter case. None of the four architectures in this document has been evaluated against demand-flexibility or schedulability at all — this is a genuine gap Phase 2 has not addressed, surfaced only by connecting Phase 1's predictability finding back to this phase's technology comparison, and worth flagging as a candidate question for any future phase rather than something resolved here.

## 8. What this means, held together

Two findings dominate this phase, and one is more important than it might first appear. The DESNZ RAAHP report is a genuinely useful primary source on domestic heating-and-cooling economics by archetype — it confirms reversible air-to-air performs best, on cost, exactly where conventional heat pumps struggle most (small flats), and worst in larger older housing stock, mirroring the shape of the property-gap case already made for Peltier technology. But its more important finding, confirmed independently by re-checking the primary PDF twice in this session, is negative: DESNZ's own dedicated 2026 study of reversible air-to-air heat pumps does not model coincident peak demand, grid impact, or time-of-use tariff interaction at all, and explicitly defers all of it to future work. That is the same gap Phase 1 found in NESO's FES 2024 baseline, now confirmed a second time in a different government document covering a different technology. The UK's cooling-demand evidence gap isn't isolated to one agency's scenario modelling — it appears to be a structural gap across the UK's official evidence base on this topic.

Set against that backdrop, no architecture in this comparison — bolted-on AC, integrated RAAHP, retrofitted air-to-water cooling, or Peltier — currently has a government-, MCS-, or HPA-verified answer to the question that actually matters: how much coincident electrical peak load does it add during the system-stress windows (heat plus low wind) that Phase 1 documented in June 2026. Nor does any of the four have a published cooling efficiency figure that would let a fair efficiency comparison be drawn between them — DESNZ doesn't report one for RAAHP, TCS doesn't report one for the Hummingbird, and no credible aggregated figure exists for standalone AC either.

**The honest conclusion for this phase is therefore not "technology X wins," but that the evidence base needed to answer that question does not yet exist publicly for any of the four architectures.** What can be said with confidence: RAAHP's best economic case (flats, per DESNZ's own figures) sits in exactly the property segment where outdoor-unit siting is hardest — a tension DESNZ's report doesn't address; a Peltier system's no-outdoor-unit design is structurally positioned to serve that same segment without that constraint, if its (currently unverified) field performance and (currently non-existent) pricing hold up; the question of whether integrating heating and cooling into one appliance reduces total grid load versus running two separate ones remains genuinely open and worth a dedicated technical follow-up; and Section 7's connection to Phase 1's mortality/predictability work adds a further, currently unaddressed dimension — none of the four architectures has been evaluated on demand flexibility or schedulability against a heat event that may be harder to forecast several days out than the equivalent winter cold event.
