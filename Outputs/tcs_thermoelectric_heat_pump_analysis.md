# Thermoelectric Heat Pumps (TCS Hummingbird/Sunbird) vs Conventional Heat Pumps: Gaps, Carbon and Cost

**Prepared by:** Andy Moran, Heaviside Analytics
**Date:** September 2026
**Status:** Draft analysis for internal review — not yet a LinkedIn post

## Purpose and scope

Thermo Electric Conversions Ltd (TCS) makes a solid-state (Peltier-effect) heat pump — no refrigerant, no compressor, no outdoor unit — marketed as the Hummingbird (domestic, ~5kW) and Sunbird (~12kW) systems. A LinkedIn contact flagged it as technology that could close installation gaps conventional air/ground source heat pumps (ASHP/GSHP) struggle with. This note asks three questions directly: (1) which UK property/installation gaps can TCS's approach address that conventional heat pumps can't or struggle to; (2) how does it compare on carbon; (3) how does it compare on running cost. Every figure below is tagged by confidence: **confirmed directly** against a primary source, **triangulated** from consistent secondary sources (not independently primary-verified), or **not verified**. No LinkedIn content has been drafted yet — this is the fact base first, per your instruction.

## 1. The gap TCS could plausibly fill

Conventional heat pumps face three well-documented deployment barriers in the UK. TCS's design addresses two of them structurally, and the evidence base for the third is currently absent.

**Installer bottleneck (F-Gas).** ASHPs and GSHPs use a refrigerant circuit, so the refrigerant-handling work in installation and servicing must be done by an F-Gas-certified engineer — a recognised constraint on how fast UK heat pump installer capacity can scale (not every task on an ASHP install requires it, but the refrigerant work is the specific bottleneck). A solid-state system with no refrigerant does not carry this requirement, which is a genuine structural advantage if TCS's systems reach market at volume. *(General mechanism confirmed — F-Gas certification is a documented UK heat pump installer bottleneck; TCS's specific installer-training pathway and current installer numbers were not found and are not verified.)*

**No outdoor unit / space-constrained properties.** ASHPs need an outdoor unit with airflow clearance and reasonable noise separation from neighbours — a hard or impossible requirement for many flats, mid-terrace properties, and dense urban housing. GSHPs need garden space or borehole access, ruling out flats entirely. A refrigerant-free, no-outdoor-unit system removes this specific constraint. This is a real and currently underserved segment, and the listed-building count is now confirmed across all four UK nations, each checked directly against its own responsible body: England 379,580 (Historic England), Scotland "around 47,000" (precisely 47,288 per HES's 2016 Scotland's Historic Environment Audit) (Historic Environment Scotland), Wales "around/over 30,000 buildings and structures" (Cadw — note this counts structures such as walls, post boxes and telephone boxes, not just buildings, so it isn't a strictly like-for-like unit with the other three nations), and Northern Ireland "over 9,100" (Department for Communities NI, updated November 2025 — supersedes an older ~9,000 figure still shown on nidirect from March 2024). Summing these gives a UK-wide total of roughly 465,000–466,000 listed buildings, with England accounting for about 82% of it — a defensible headline figure if a "UK-wide ~465,000" framing is useful, though the cross-nation sum should be treated as approximate given the counting-convention differences (England: listed building "entries"; Wales: buildings *and* structures; Scotland: address-based). A large stock of solid-wall, largely pre-1919 properties also poses retrofit difficulty for heat pumps generally (**triangulated**, not separately isolated from listed-building figures for England).

**Built-in cooling.** TCS's own product page states directly that "Hummingbird leverages the solid-state Peltier effect to both heat and cool, eliminating refrigerants and compressors" — **confirmed directly** from teconversion.com. This is a genuine structural feature of the technology, not a marketing add-on: Peltier devices are inherently reversible (the hot and cold sides swap when current direction is reversed), so heating-and-cooling in one unit comes essentially for free with the underlying physics. Crucially, this is not universal across heat pumps, so it is a real point of differentiation rather than something every competitor already offers. Air-to-air heat pumps (the split-unit type) are typically reversible and do double as air conditioning — but the dominant UK-installed type is air-to-water, feeding radiators or underfloor heating, and **confirmed directly** from an installer-industry source: "many air-to-water heat pumps in the UK are designed solely for space heating and hot water, without the capacity to cool indoor air," with cooling on those systems requiring additional hardware (fan coil units) that is described as costly to retrofit rather than standard. So a household replacing a gas boiler with a typical UK air-to-water ASHP usually gains heating only; the same household with a TCS system would gain both, with no extra kit. This also connects to the demand-side/grid work elsewhere in this project: a technology that can shift to serving cooling load during a heatwave is a different, and currently unexamined, peak-demand story from the winter-heating-only comparison the rest of this analysis focuses on — worth flagging as a possible follow-on thread rather than analysing here, since no UK heatwave-cooling-demand data has been gathered for this document.

**What the evidence does *not* yet establish:** that TCS's technology performs adequately, at competitive running cost, in these gap-filling contexts. That is precisely what sections 2–3 test — a technology that fills an installation gap but costs or emits substantially more per unit of heat delivered is filling the gap at a real trade-off, not for free.

## 2. Carbon performance comparison

**Method:** kgCO2e per kWh of *delivered heat*, calculated as (1 ÷ efficiency or CoP) × the relevant fuel carbon-intensity factor. This isolates the heating technology's carbon performance from how much heat a building needs.

**Carbon intensity factors used (DESNZ/Defra, 2026 conversion factors):**
- UK grid electricity, generation basis: 0.13096 kgCO2e/kWh — **confirmed directly**, read from the primary 2026 conversion-factors spreadsheet's "UK Electricity" tab (0.12943 CO2 + 0.00067 CH4 + 0.00086 N2O = 0.13096 total kgCO2e/kWh, "Electricity generated" row, 2026). Automated fetching of the PDF version of this same table had earlier produced inconsistent extractions; this was resolved once the actual spreadsheet row was read directly.
- UK grid electricity, T&D losses: 0.01299 kgCO2e/kWh — **confirmed directly**, same spreadsheet, "T&D — UK electricity" row (0.01284 CO2 + 0.00007 CH4 + 0.00009 N2O = 0.01299 total, 2026).
- UK grid electricity, T&D-loss-adjusted / "consumed" (generation + T&D losses): 0.13096 + 0.01299 = **0.14395 kgCO2e/kWh** — **confirmed directly**, both components as above. (Displayed as ~0.144 in the tables below; the precise value rounds to the same figures throughout.)
- Natural gas combustion, gross CV basis: **0.18231 kgCO2e/kWh** — **confirmed directly**, read from the primary 2026 conversion-factors spreadsheet's "Fuels" tab ("Natural gas," "kWh (Gross CV)" row: 0.18194 CO2 + 0.00028 CH4 + 0.00009 N2O = 0.18231 total, 2026). This resolves the earlier discrepancy: the 0.18296 figure repeated across two secondary sources was wrong; 0.18231 is correct. All carbon-intensity figures used in this analysis are now confirmed directly against the primary spreadsheet.

**Efficiency/CoP figures used:**
- Gas combi boiler, real-world (in-situ monitored, not nameplate): 82.5% — **confirmed directly**, DECC/government in-situ monitoring trial of condensing boilers. (Nameplate SEDBUK rating for regular boilers was 90.4%, but the same trial found real installed performance running ~5 points below nameplate for regular boilers and is not directly comparable but consistently lower for combis — using the real-world figure, not the marketing/nameplate figure, deliberately, for the same reason the TCS CoP is treated with care below.)
- ASHP, real-world SCOP, conservative/representative: 2.78 — **confirmed directly**, DESNZ Electrification of Heat trial (government-run, representative sample).
- ASHP, real-world SCOP, best observed practice: 3.83 — **confirmed directly**, heatpumpmonitor.org (large monitored-fleet dataset, but skews toward well-installed/enthusiast systems, so treated as an upper bound rather than typical).
- GSHP, real-world SPF: **3.25** (median 3.26 [IQR 2.83–3.64], mean 3.24 [95% CI 3.16–3.32]) — **confirmed directly**, from an analysis of Ofgem RHI metering data 2017–2022 covering 286 GSHP installations (published via the Ground Source Heat Pump Association/RECC, and cited by DESNZ's own Electrification of Heat report as the more robust GSHP benchmark — that trial's own GSHP sample, 23 usable properties out of 38 installed, was explicitly flagged as too small to use). One caveat carried from the source itself: these are RHI-metered homes specifically, which the researchers note may be atypical (bivalent heating systems, second homes with unusual usage patterns), and the metering boundary only approximately matches the SEPEMO H4 standard used elsewhere in this analysis — a solid figure, not a perfect one, but a large step up from having no figure at all.
- Direct electric resistance heating (panel heaters, and — relevant to this project's other work — storage heaters, approximately): CoP = 1.0 — uncontroversial, physics-determined.
- TCS Hummingbird, **marketed** CoP: reported as ">10" in TCS's own marketing and in Energy Voice coverage.
- TCS Hummingbird, **verified final commercialised system** CoP: approximately 4.9 (up from ~4.5 earlier in development). **Confirmed directly** from a DESNZ Heat Pump Ready Programme funding document (the same document that reports MCS certification is still in progress, not achieved — see Section 3). The same document reports an early experimental prototype hit CoP 12, which is very likely a favourable-condition lab figure, not representative of the fielded system. The gap between the ">10" marketing claim and the ~4.9 figure DESNZ's own funding review reports is large and material — it should not be repeated uncritically in any public-facing content. **Important metric caveat:** this ~4.9 is a CoP — a single-condition instantaneous efficiency figure, almost certainly measured at a favourable test condition — not a SCOP measured across a full heating season the way the ASHP figures above are. Every ASHP figure in this table is a *seasonal* average; the TCS figure is not. Even if 4.9 survives field installation perfectly, a genuine seasonal figure for TCS would likely sit below it, the same way ASHP nameplate/rated figures sit above ASHP field SCOPs. The comparison below should be read with that metric mismatch in mind, not as CoP-to-CoP or SCOP-to-SCOP like-for-like.

**Comparison table — kgCO2e per kWh of delivered heat:**

| Technology | Input basis | Carbon intensity used | kgCO2e / kWh heat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gas combi boiler (real-world) | 1.212 kWh gas per kWh heat (82.5% eff.) | 0.18231 | **0.221** |
| Direct electric resistance / storage heater | 1.0 kWh elec per kWh heat | 0.144 (T&D-adjusted) | **0.144** |
| ASHP, conservative real-world (SCOP 2.78) | 0.360 kWh elec per kWh heat | 0.144 | **0.052** |
| ASHP, best-practice (SCOP 3.83) | 0.261 kWh elec per kWh heat | 0.144 | **0.038** |
| GSHP, real-world (SPF 3.25) | 0.308 kWh elec per kWh heat | 0.144 | **0.044** |
| TCS Hummingbird, verified CoP ~4.9 | 0.204 kWh elec per kWh heat | 0.144 | **0.029** |

*(The TCS ">10" marketed-CoP figure is deliberately not shown as a row here, even bracketed — it's a marketing claim with a different, undisclosed test basis, and including it invites screenshots without the caveat attached.)*

**Reading this table honestly:** on the verified CoP (~4.9, with the seasonal-vs-instantaneous caveat above), TCS's carbon performance per unit of delivered heat is *better* than a conservative real-world ASHP and roughly comparable to a well-installed one — a genuinely strong result, and one that holds even before crediting the marketing figure. It is dramatically better than resistance/storage heating or gas. Two further caveats worth stating explicitly: first, the absolute figures here will keep falling as the grid decarbonises further — the durable result is the *relative* ranking between technologies, not the absolute kgCO2e numbers, which are a snapshot of 2026 grid intensity. Second, the comparison is asymmetric in a way that favours gas rather than disadvantages it: the electricity figure includes transmission & distribution losses (upstream of the meter), while the gas figure is combustion-only and excludes upstream methane leakage and well-to-tank emissions. Correcting that asymmetry would widen every electric technology's advantage over gas, not narrow it — so the gas row here is, if anything, flattered.

## 3. Cost performance comparison

**Retail unit prices used:** Ofgem price cap, October–December 2026, GB average, direct debit — **confirmed directly** from Ofgem's own published unit-rate page: electricity 26.32 p/kWh, gas 7.97 p/kWh (5% VAT). These are capped default-tariff rates; fixed-deal rates can differ in either direction and are not modelled here.

Electricity carries 0% VAT for this period, but that is a **temporary** measure, not a permanent change — **confirmed directly** against HMRC's Revenue and Customs Brief 10/2026: the zero rate runs 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027 only, applies to electricity alone, and gas remains at 5% throughout. Any figure or claim built on this VAT treatment needs revisiting after March 2027.

These per-kWh figures also omit standing charges, which matter for a household considering dropping gas entirely: the Oct–Dec 2026 cap sets the gas standing charge at 29.68p/day. A household that goes fully electric (TCS, ASHP, or resistance heating) and disconnects gas avoids that charge entirely — a fixed saving that further improves the economics of every all-electric option relative to keeping a gas boiler, on top of the per-kWh comparison below.

**Comparison table — running cost, pence per kWh of delivered heat:**

| Technology | Input per kWh heat | Unit price | p / kWh heat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gas combi boiler (real-world 82.5%) | 1.212 kWh gas | 7.97p | **9.66p** |
| Direct electric resistance / storage heater | 1.0 kWh elec | 26.32p | **26.32p** |
| ASHP, conservative real-world (SCOP 2.78) | 0.360 kWh elec | 26.32p | **9.47p** |
| ASHP, best-practice (SCOP 3.83) | 0.261 kWh elec | 26.32p | **6.87p** |
| GSHP, real-world (SPF 3.25) | 0.308 kWh elec | 26.32p | **8.10p** |
| TCS Hummingbird, verified CoP ~4.9 | 0.204 kWh elec | 26.32p | **5.37p** |

**Three findings worth being direct about:**

The most quotable one first, and it's not about TCS at all: at current cap rates, a conservative real-world ASHP (9.47p/kWh heat) is essentially at cost parity with a real-world gas boiler (9.66p/kWh heat) — a striking, counter-narrative result given the common claim that heat pumps cost more to run, and it holds independently of anything TCS-related. It also sharpens what TCS would actually add on top: not "beating gas," which a conservative ASHP already does, but a further ~42% running-cost improvement over that ASHP (5.37p vs 9.47p).

That near-parity result is ratio-dependent in a way the other comparisons aren't. It rests on the current ~3.3:1 electricity-to-gas price ratio, which is genuinely volatile — the next price cap review could move it materially in either direction. The ASHP-vs-TCS comparison and the resistance-heating comparison don't share this fragility, since they compare electricity to electricity at a fixed ratio of 1. Any version of this analysis that ages past the next cap review should recheck this ratio before repeating the "near parity" framing.

On TCS specifically: on a like-for-like verified-performance basis, its running cost per kWh of heat would be the *cheapest* of everything in this table — cheaper than a well-installed ASHP and roughly half the cost of a modern gas boiler. If the CoP ~4.9 figure holds up in real installed, seasonal conditions (not just the DESNZ-reported development trial, and bearing in mind the CoP-vs-SCOP mismatch noted in Section 2), that is a genuinely strong result and the single most important number in this analysis. It should be stated with its confidence level attached, not as an established fact for fielded systems.

Finally, upfront cost is where the comparison currently breaks down, and this matters as much as running cost. TCS has **no public pricing** for Hummingbird or Sunbird — this was confirmed as an explicit finding (no listed price found anywhere), so no capital-cost or payback comparison can currently be made against an ASHP (which typically has BUS-grant-supported installed costs in the ~£7,000–£13,000 range net of grant). Critically, TCS is **not currently MCS-certified** — confirmed directly, in this analysis, from the same DESNZ funding document reporting the CoP figures, which states MCS certification efforts are "underway" as of January 2025, not achieved. MCS certification is a hard prerequisite for the £7,500 Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant. Unless and until TCS achieves MCS certification, a household buying a Hummingbird system today would not be eligible for that grant, which would need to be added back into any real cost comparison against a grant-supported ASHP installation.

## 4. What this means, held together

TCS's thermoelectric approach has a coherent, evidenced case on four fronts at once: it removes a real installer bottleneck (no F-Gas requirement), it removes a real installation barrier (no outdoor unit, relevant to flats and listed/heritage buildings), it provides built-in cooling as a structural byproduct of the Peltier effect (where most UK-installed air-to-water ASHPs don't, without extra hardware), and — on the manufacturer's own DESNZ-reported figures for the commercialised system (CoP ~4.9, not the marketed >10 claim) — it would out-perform a typical real-world ASHP, and a real-world GSHP (SPF 3.25), on both carbon and running cost per unit of heat delivered. GSHP is the closest competitor on these figures, sitting between the two ASHP scenarios — but GSHP needs garden space or a borehole, which is exactly the constraint TCS's no-outdoor-unit design is positioned to avoid, so even where GSHP performs well it isn't addressing the same property gap. That combination, if it holds up, would be a genuinely significant development for exactly the property types this project's earlier work (storage heaters, VPP, hard-to-treat housing) has focused on.

The case is not yet provable at the level this project holds itself to, for three specific reasons: (1) the ~4.9 CoP is the best figure DESNZ's own funding review reports for the commercialised design, but it has not been independently field-monitored the way ASHP performance has via heatpumpmonitor.org or the Electrification of Heat trial — it may not survive contact with real installed conditions any better than ASHP nameplate SCOPs survive contact with reality; (2) there is no public price, so the economics of actually buying and installing one cannot be assessed, only the cost of running it once installed; (3) it is not MCS-certified, so today it is not BUS-grant-eligible, which matters a great deal for a like-for-like cost comparison against a grant-supported ASHP.

**Recommended framing if this becomes a LinkedIn post:** the honest, defensible version of this story is "a promising technology that appears to solve two real structural problems and shows strong performance on the manufacturer's own figures — but isn't yet verified in the field, isn't priced, and isn't grant-eligible" — not "a breakthrough that beats heat pumps." The CoP >10 marketing claim should not be repeated; the ~4.9 figure, correctly sourced to the DESNZ document, is still a strong and creditable number and doesn't need inflating.
