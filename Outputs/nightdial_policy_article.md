The lowest-carbon heating in this project isn't a heat pump. It's a bar heater, a blanket, and being cold. That single fact should unsettle anyone who thinks the market is pricing this correctly.

**A two-year-old child, a legal deadline, and a £1.4bn bill**

Awaab Ishak was two years old when he died from mould exposure in a Rochdale housing association flat in 2020. Children carry a disproportionate share of the risk that cold, damp homes carry for everyone: cold homes cost the NHS £540 million a year in England alone, and poor housing more broadly — cold, damp, and the mould that follows — costs £1.4 billion a year in first-year treatment costs, before counting the damage to the housing stock itself or a lifetime of chronic health effects. The law that followed Awaab's death — requiring social landlords to treat excess cold as a legal hazard, not a lifestyle choice — comes into force this November.

That deadline is real and close. What isn't yet real is a market that makes meeting it affordable. This piece is about why: not because the technology is missing, but because the prices tenants and landlords actually face are shaped by policy choices that nobody designed on purpose, and that are entirely possible to undo.

**The paradox this project keeps finding**

Across the 40 real households I've been using throughout this series, three ways of delivering the same heat produce three very different carbon outcomes. Storage heating, run as people actually run it, cuts carbon by 29% against gas; run perfectly, by 35%. A heat pump at real field performance cuts carbon by 77% — a quarter of gas, and two-thirds lower again than even storage heating's best case.

If the market priced carbon the way it prices everything else, the technologies that protect health and cut emissions would also be the cheapest to run. They aren't. Gas costs £418 a year for the same heat. Storage heating costs £719 a year even optimised, £964 run badly. A heat pump on an ordinary electricity tariff costs £470 — more than gas. Only on a heat-pump-specific tariff does the cost line finally agree with the carbon line, at £302.

That gap between what's cleanest and what's cheapest isn't a rounding error or a temporary market wobble. It's the direct, traceable result of how gas and electricity are priced in the UK today — and once you look at where that pricing comes from, it stops looking accidental.

**Why gas wins on price: it isn't carbon-priced**

The UK already has a real, live carbon price. The UK Emissions Trading Scheme currently prices carbon at £58.27 a tonne, and it applies to power generation, heavy industry, and aviation. It does not apply to domestic gas heating. A gas boiler burning fuel in someone's home emits exactly the same CO₂ as a power station burning the equivalent gas — but only one of them pays for it.

The number that follows is a useful gut-check. A household's gas heating emits 958 kg of CO₂ a year. Apply the UK's own existing carbon price to that figure, exactly as it's already applied to gas power generation, and it adds £55.83 to the annual gas bill. That's not a hypothetical new tax — it's the price the UK has already decided carbon is worth, just never charged to this specific use of gas. And it very nearly closes the entire £52-a-year gap between gas at £418 and a heat pump on an ordinary tariff at £470, without a special tariff, a subsidy, or a grant scheme anywhere in the calculation.

It gets more lopsided from there. The policy levies meant to fund the transition penalise the cleaner fuel by a factor of three. Environmental and policy costs already loaded onto energy bills — the levies that fund renewable subsidies, energy efficiency schemes, and the like — make up 16% of the average electricity bill, but only 5.5% of the average gas bill. Put another way: 82% of all levy revenue is collected from electricity, and just 18% from gas, even though UK households burn roughly three times more gas than electricity across the year. The fuel that's cleaner is carrying most of the policy cost. The fuel that isn't priced for its carbon at all is carrying almost none of it.

**Why the inversion happens: one generator sets everyone's price**

Because gas sets the wholesale electricity price roughly 97% of the time — Great Britain's market pays every generator whatever the last, marginal generator needed to meet demand is charging — the ETS carbon cost gas power stations pay gets baked into every unit of electricity sold, including wind and solar. Gas boilers burning the same fuel for heat pay nothing. Same fuel, two prices, and no conspiracy behind it: just a carbon price applied to one use of gas and never extended to the other.

**The tariff fix that already exists — and the one that doesn't**

Here's where the story gets more hopeful, at least for one of the two technologies in this comparison. On an ordinary electricity tariff, a real-world heat pump (field-measured SCOP 2.78, from DESNZ's 428-household Electrification of Heat trial) needs to hit SCOP 3.49 just to break even with gas — and loses for every one of the 40 real households in this project. Put the identical hardware on a heat-pump-specific tariff — Economy 7, or a purpose-built one like Octopus Cosy — and the breakeven point drops to SCOP 2.24. The same real 2.78 now beats gas for all 40 households. Nothing about the heat pump changed. The billing structure underneath it did.

That's a genuinely solvable problem, and it's already been solved — those tariffs exist today. The barrier left for heat pumps isn't inventing a fix, it's making sure the households who need it are actually on it.

Storage heating's path is different — and harder. Closing its own gap to gas through the tariff alone would need roughly a 42% cut to the off-peak rate, down to around 8.85p/kWh. That's not a modest adjustment; it's likely below the combined wholesale-and-network cost of supplying the electricity in the first place, which means no supplier could offer it and stay solvent. Different technology, different physics, different answer — and pretending otherwise would just set up a promise nobody can keep.

**What actually has to change**

None of what's driving this gap is a law of nature. Each piece is a specific, named, human decision, which means each one is a specific, nameable thing that could be decided differently.

Rebalance the levies. The 16%-versus-5.5% split between electricity and gas bills has been flagged as overdue for correction by Nesta and the MCS Foundation, among others — a known fix, costed and proposed, just not yet implemented.

Extend the Boiler Upgrade Scheme to social housing. Its April 2026 refresh extended the scheme to 2030 and added a new air-to-air grant, yet social housing remains excluded outright, regardless of heat pump type — the single largest capex blocker sitting in front of exactly the housing stock this series has been about.

Fund the installer pipeline, at the scale the gap actually requires. Roughly 3,000 qualified heat pump engineers are active in the UK today; government's own targets imply needing around 27,000 by 2028, and around 62,000 by 2035 — a ninefold increase inside six years, not a gradual scale-up. That gap doesn't close by wishing it away, and it won't close on the current trajectory either: only about 23% of heating trades professionals are ASHP-trained today, and existing gas engineers have little financial reason to retrain, since it costs them up to £2,000 with no wage premium waiting on the other side. Closing this gap needs training investment and a genuine incentive to retrain, sized to match a ninefold workforce increase rather than a modest top-up.

And price gas heating's carbon honestly — or pair the alternative directly with a safeguard for the households who can't absorb it. Either extend something like the ETS logic to domestic gas combustion, which would go most of the way to closing the cost gap on its own, or take the equivalent number — £55.83 a household a year — and use it to fund a targeted, means-tested tariff or transfer for the people this piece opened with: the households already paying for a mispriced market in cold, not cash. Doing the first without the second punishes exactly the people least able to absorb a gas price rise. Doing the second without the first leaves the market signal broken indefinitely. Neither works well alone.

**The bottom line**

Every one of these four is a policy choice, not a fact about physics or a limitation of the hardware. That's the whole point of writing this piece: the households paying for this market's mistakes with their health didn't create the mistake, and they can't fix it by themselves. The people who set electricity levies, funding rules, and training budgets can.
