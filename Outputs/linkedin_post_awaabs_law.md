For the first time, one UK law puts "too cold" and "too hot" under the same repair clock. For social housing providers that's a genuinely different technology-selection question arriving faster than most retrofit planning cycles.

From 30 November 2026, Awaab's Law — already well known for forcing social landlords to address damp and mould on a fixed timescale — brings excess cold and excess heat into the same statutory regime, alongside hazards like falls and fire.

Putting cold and heat under one framework changes the technology question, not just the compliance question.

A landlord who now has to address both within the same statutory framework has a reason to stop thinking about heating and cooling as two separate jobs — a heating system here, a portable cooling fix there — and start asking whether one installed system can do both.

That's a real shift in how a retrofit gets specified, especially where a landlord is already planning one.

An integrated system — capable of heating and cooling from the same installed architecture — could have a genuine operational and economic advantage in that scenario.

I want to be precise about what kind of claim this is: it's my own inference from the structure of the regulation, not a government recommendation. The guidance itself points landlords towards a mix of measures for heat, including ventilation and shading, rather than towards any particular technology.

The mechanics, for context: Awaab's Law Phase 1 (October 2025) covered emergency hazards and damp/mould. Phase 2, from 30 November 2026, extends the same fixed-timeframe regime to excess cold, excess heat, and several other hazard categories.

Once a hazard is identified, a landlord has 10 working days to investigate, 3 to give written findings, and 5 to start safety work — 24 hours for anything emergency-grade.

One qualification worth being upfront about: this doesn't mean every hot home becomes an Awaab's Law case.

Government guidance is explicit that excess heat only counts where it stems from a deficiency in the property that the landlord is responsible for. Overheating that is simply the result of unusually hot weather is not in scope, though landlords are still encouraged to think about climate adaptation more broadly.

30 November 2026 is now less than three months away.

For social housing providers planning retrofits over the next 12–18 months, the question is shifting from:

"How do we replace the heating?"

to:

"How do we make this home resilient at both ends of the temperature range, in a way that works within the new hazard regime?"

That's a genuinely different technology-selection question — and it's arriving faster than most retrofit planning cycles are built for.

For clarity: this currently applies to social housing in England only. The Renters' Rights Act 2025 gives powers to extend it to the private rented sector, but that's a separate, currently undated question.
