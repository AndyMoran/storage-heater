The UK's electricity-system planning may be underestimating one of the fastest-growing sources of summer demand: cooling.

NESO's Future Energy Scenarios still assumes fixed ambient temperatures in its modelling, with no future warming built into the core assumptions. And its published workbooks don't expose a separate future trajectory for domestic air-conditioning demand.

That matters because the evidence that UK cooling demand is changing is no longer particularly speculative.

The Met Office calls a night where the minimum temperature stays at or above 20°C a "tropical night." The current probability of three consecutive tropical nights in July is around 20%, compared with under 1% in the pre-industrial climate. Nationally, at least one tropical night has occurred in all but three years this century, compared with roughly half of all years between 1961 and 2000.

And this isn't just about uncomfortable bedrooms.

UKHSA's interim assessment attributes 2,877 excess deaths to the May and June 2026 heat episodes alone — nearly twice the 1,504 recorded across the whole of summer 2025.

A separate analysis for the Climate Change Committee estimates that, at today's temperatures, extreme heat already causes around 850 excess deaths, 17,000 excess A&E attendances and 2,900 emergency admissions in the UK each year. Under a central 2°C warming scenario, it estimates around 3,200 excess deaths and 53,000 excess A&E attendances annually by the 2050s.

Then there's sleep.

A 2026 peer-reviewed study estimated that suboptimal nighttime temperatures cost the average person in the UK around 1,217 minutes of sleep per year between 2011 and 2023, with an associated economic cost of roughly 0.04–0.05% of GDP annually.

Yet only around 3–5% of UK homes have air conditioning, compared with roughly 25% in France, 60% in Italy and 70–80% in Greece.

So we have an interesting asymmetry.

The UK has very little installed cooling capacity precisely as the climate is producing more conditions in which households may want it.

And some of the measures that improve winter energy efficiency can make summer overheating harder to manage if they aren't accompanied by adequate shading and ventilation.

If cooling adoption rises, that's not just a household technology story. It becomes an electricity-system planning question: when thousands or millions of homes respond to the same heat event by switching on cooling, where does that demand appear in the system model?

At present, it isn't clearly visible in NESO's published FES demand trajectories.

That looks less like a rounding error and more like a material analytical gap.

The question isn't whether every UK home will suddenly install air conditioning.

It's whether our electricity planning is properly testing what happens if cooling demand starts behaving like a normal household load — precisely because the climate is making the underlying need more normal.
