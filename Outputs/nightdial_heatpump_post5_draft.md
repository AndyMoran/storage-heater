Heat pumps cut carbon by more than three-quarters against gas. Storage heaters beat it too — but by far less, and it took two rounds of checking my own working to get that number right.

I've spent this project checking whether storage heaters are the right fix for this housing stock. The honest answer, checked against real, demand-weighted 2026 figures rather than a spec sheet or a simple average, is no — a heat pump is. Here's the carbon case, the cleanest of the three I looked at.

Take the real average heat demand across the 40 confirmed households I've been using all along: about 4,720 kWh of heat needed per household per winter. Three ways to deliver it, each checked against a real, non-cherry-picked, wind-balanced sample of nights spanning the full range of temperatures that actually carry this demand, with each household-night matched to the real carbon intensity that goes with it:

Gas: 958 kg CO2 a year, using the real efficiency-adjusted intensity of gas heat (203 gCO2 per kWh).

Storage heating, as people actually run it: 678 kg — a real 29% saving against gas. Run it perfectly — charged to exact need, no waste — and it drops to 621 kg. Both are genuine wins over gas, and both held up under scrutiny I didn't expect to need: an earlier pass on this same calculation had a subtle bias in it — cold nights that also happened to be unusually still (low wind, which drives up grid carbon intensity) were overrepresented in the sample, making one temperature band look artificially dirty. Once corrected for a fair mix of windy and calm nights at every temperature, storage heating's real advantage over gas is larger than I'd first reported, not smaller — and steadier, too: I still keep a separate, explicitly worst-case figure from the coldest, stillest real nights I could find (35% of those specifically adverse nights individually beat gas on carbon), because a genuinely bad stretch of weather is still a real thing that happens. But it's a worst case, not the average a household should expect.

Heat pump, at real measured field performance rather than a manufacturer's number (SCOP 2.78, DESNZ's 428-household Electrification of Heat trial): the same 4,720 kWh of heat needs only 1,698 kWh of electricity. Against that same real, wind-balanced sample, that comes to 223 kg CO2 — under a quarter of gas, and two-thirds of even storage heating's best case. Because it draws so much less power per unit of warmth, no bin of real nights I checked pushed it anywhere near gas, even at the high end. Of the 40 households in the dataset, not one individually crosses gas's carbon intensity on a demand-weighted basis.

On running cost, the honest caveat still stands: that same real-world heat pump only beats gas on a heat-pump-specific tariff (Economy 7 or Octopus Cosy). On an ordinary rate, it currently loses. Carbon and cost are answering different questions, and today they don't point the same way for every household — which matters for what comes next.

Because the actual barrier here was never physics. It's price design, an installer shortage, a funding scheme that excludes social housing outright, and a few tenure barriers layered on top. Every one of those is a policy choice, not a law of nature. Next: what has to change to fix it.
