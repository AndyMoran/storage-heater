## Post 1 — What a £35m island trial still has to say about a £1.65bn national problem

SSEN spent £34.95m on NINES, a decade-long Shetland trial testing whether flexible domestic demand — smart storage heaters, hot water tanks, a battery — could soak up wind power the grid would otherwise waste.

The headline results were real: 8.5MW of new renewable generation connected, diesel use down about 10%, 234 homes responding to grid frequency in milliseconds. But dig into the project's own economics report and there's a number nobody talks about: the flexible demand alleviated an estimated 105.7 MWh of wind curtailment a year, at a cost of £5,364.75/MWh. Even the theoretical best case — perfect timing between charging and curtailment — was £560.94/MWh. Wholesale power trades in the tens of pounds per MWh. Recruitment struggled too: the plan was 750 homes, Shetland Islands Council pulled out in 2012, and the trial rolled out to 234. The project's own Close-Down Report is blunt about why: "the speed of recruitment of DSM... is extremely slow in comparison to conventional reinforcement," and "the underlying fixed costs (in particular communications) make small scale demand side management... uneconomic" — net comms and support cost ran £330–380 per house per year, and the report says fixing that "requires an order of magnitude reduction."

That question — could flexible demand substitute for a bigger wire — never got compared against the £641.8m interconnector that was later built to solve Shetland's power problem, most likely because a large offshore-adjacent wind farm, Viking, needed that link to export its own power before its subsidy deadline. Priorities changed mid-story; the original demand-side question became beside the point for that decision. It's also worth naming a second, separate reason the two were never on the same footing: NINES was costed in £/MWh of curtailment avoided, the interconnector in £/MW of capacity delivered — different units that were never converted onto one ruler.

This isn't really a battery-cost problem. Storage prices have fallen over 90% since NINES was designed, but cheaper batteries don't touch a recruitment and comms-overhead problem. What they do mean: the storage side of this comparison is far less of a barrier than in 2013, making its continued absence harder to justify, not easier. NINES didn't fail — it proved active network management works and connected 8.5MW of renewables.

And the underlying question is no longer Shetland-sized: Viking is now curtailing roughly 60% of its own output because the Scotland-England transmission corridor is full, and GB-wide constraint payments hit £1.65 billion in 15 months. A battery there can't export past a full boundary any more than the wind farm can — but it could time-shift generation into hours when the boundary has headroom. Nobody's published a costed answer to that question either, at Viking's scale or the UK's.

*(2,856 characters)*

**Caveats to keep:** the curtailment figures trace to the project's own 6A Commercial Arrangements and Economics Report and SSEN's 2017 results release (not the 2A Battery report, which doesn't contain the 105.7 MWh/£5,364.75 figures); the two recruitment/comms quotes are from a separate document, the project's own Close-Down Report — both confirmed by direct document fetch, both real, verbatim quotes. The Viking-as-driver explanation is this note's own inference bridging two separate regulatory documents (Baringa's appraisal, which doesn't name Viking, and Ofgem's later Needs Case decision, which does) — say "most likely explanation," never "the confirmed reason." Viking's curtailment is a downstream Scotland-England transmission bottleneck plus maintenance unavailability, not a sign the Shetland interconnector itself was undersized or failed at its job. The £1.65bn/£116m figures are GB-wide totals across many curtailed generators, not Viking-specific — present as system-scale context. Viking's capacity is reported inconsistently across sources (457MW, 443MW, 433MW, 370MW) — avoid quoting a precise figure. This post argues two related but distinct things at once — the comparison never happened, and NINES's own numbers weren't compelling on their own terms either — don't let one point overshadow or seem to cancel the other. The "ANM + modern storage, minus domestic DSM" framing is this note's own proposed reframing of the live question, not a comparison anyone has actually run or published — present it as a question worth asking, not a result.

## Post 2 — The per-home cost ladder

Three real numbers, three different scales, same underlying question.

Retrofitting a storage heater with smart load control: roughly £400–£780 per home (real 2026 Scottish social housing deployments — Cambuslang, Angus, West Dunbartonshire).

Converting a home to a heat pump: at least £5,000 per home (Eildon Housing Association's own capital estimate).

Reinforcing a substation to carry that heat pump load at scale: £7.5m, for one 69-unit development (Maiden Hill).

None of these numbers tells you which option is "right" for a given home or development — they're solving different problems, at different points in a building's life. But laid next to each other, they're a useful reminder of how differently "electrify heat" can be priced depending on where in the chain you intervene, and how rarely those price points get compared in the same room.

*(856 characters)*

**Caveats to keep:** these are three different interventions solving different problems (a control retrofit doesn't decarbonise heat; a heat pump does; a substation upgrade is a consequence of adding load) — don't let the post imply they're interchangeable alternatives, only that they sit at very different points on a cost ladder that's rarely discussed as one ladder.

## Post 3 — The same island, six years later

Six years after the interconnector appraisal, SSEN is consulting on Shetland's network again — the Gremista GSP Strategic Development Plan.

It says secondary transformers in the north of the supply area are projected to overload by 2028. It says local flexibility "is likely to be required alongside traditional reinforcement." It even notes that more vulnerable households may have less ability to provide that flexibility in the first place.

It doesn't cost flexibility as an alternative to reinforcement. Only as a supporting measure once reinforcement is already decided.

Same island. Same operator. Same shape of gap. This isn't a historical curiosity from 2019 — it's happening again, right now, in a live consultation.

**Caveats to keep:** the Gremista plan is an earlier, lighter-weight planning/consultation document than the 2019 Baringa appraisal, not a formal multi-hundred-million-pound investment decision on the same scale — be precise that it's the same *pattern* repeating, not literally "another £600m decision," or a technically-minded reader will push back on the comparison.

## Post 4 — The hopeful counterpoint

While Shetland's formal appraisals were leaving demand-side options out of the comparison, something else was happening 200 miles south.

Kight PowerHub is opening Scotland's first domestic battery-storage factory, in Dumfries — designed over six years with South of Scotland Enterprise, built with feedback directly from social housing landlords. Up to 700 jobs, plus 2,000 more across the supply chain. Units are already installed in social housing, with the company reporting an average 80% cut in tenant electricity bills (their own figure — I haven't seen it independently audited, but the direction is notable).

The negawatt side of this argument isn't theoretical any more. It's being manufactured, for exactly the households this whole conversation is meant to help.

*(775 characters)*

**Caveats to keep:** the 80% bill-reduction figure is Kight's own claim, not independently verified — always attribute it to the company when quoting it, never state it as an established fact; the battery chemistry Kight uses ("NGA," non-graphite anode) is only confirmed in one source so far and shouldn't be stated with more confidence than that.

## Post 5 — The question NINES never measured

NINES's evaluation reports measure curtailment avoided, capacity connected, cost per MWh. What none of them measure: whether the 234 homes in the trial ended up any warmer, or any cheaper to heat.

That's not a small gap. Shetland's fuel poverty rate is 40% — one of the highest of any Scottish local authority, against a 31% national average, behind only Orkney's 51% (Scottish Government, Local Authority Tables, 2022–2024). Separately, 1,854 people in Shetland received emergency fuel vouchers in under three years, including 614 children (Shetland Times, Oct 2025).

SSEN's own current strategy for Shetland names fuel poverty as priority one. NINES — a project that physically altered the heating systems of several hundred Shetland homes less than a decade earlier — isn't mentioned in it anywhere.

This doesn't mean NINES failed at something it was never designed to do; it was a grid-capacity trial, not a welfare intervention, and should be judged on that basis. But if a NINES-style trial were designed today, in a place this fuel-poor, it's fair to ask why tenant bills and comfort wouldn't be measured alongside curtailment. That's a second, separate question this case study raises — not a substitute for the first.

*(1,229 characters)*

**Caveats to keep:** the 40%/31% fuel poverty figures are confirmed directly from the Scottish Government's own Local Authority Tables press release (26 May 2026); the 1,854/614 voucher figures are confirmed via the Shetland Times and corroborated by the Fuel Bank Foundation's own account. Don't add a specific "extreme fuel poverty" percentage for Shetland — it couldn't be independently confirmed and shouldn't be quoted. Don't imply NINES caused or worsened fuel poverty, or that it should have been designed as an anti-poverty scheme — the honest claim is narrower: its own evaluation never measured this outcome, on a population where it plainly matters.

## Article draft — "The £35 Million Trial That Never Made It Into the £600 Million Decision"

*Suggested use: LinkedIn article/newsletter, or a short technical note for NESO/Ofgem/DNO audiences. ~1,450 words.*

In 2011, Shetland had a problem that sounds almost quaint today: too much wind. The island's electrical system — cut off from the mainland grid, running on diesel generation and a single wind farm — was already at the edge of what it could stably absorb. Wind on Shetland has a load factor of around 52%, nearly double the UK average, and most of that resource was going to waste because the network couldn't take any more of it without risking the kind of instability that causes blackouts.

SSEN's answer was Northern Isles New Energy Solutions, or NINES: a £34.95m trial, funded through Ofgem's Low Carbon Networks Fund, that spent the best part of a decade testing whether controlling demand — not just generation — could let the island absorb more renewable power without new infrastructure. The centrepiece was domestic: up to 1,000 homes fitted with redesigned smart storage heaters and controlled hot water tanks, developed with Glen Dimplex, capable of responding to grid frequency in around 50 milliseconds. Alongside them sat a 1MW/3MWh battery at Lerwick Power Station — reportedly the largest lead-acid installation in Europe at the time — and an active network management system controlling everything from small domestic loads up to multi-megawatt wind farms.

By the numbers, it worked. Renewable generation connected to the network rose from two turbines to 8.5MW. In one month in 2017, 31% of the island's electricity came from renewables. SSEN's own Head of Innovation, presenting the results at an industry conference that year, was candid that the trial's headline contributions — 1.5MW of demand flexibility from 234 homes, a 1MW battery — were "not a big proportion" of total island demand, framing NINES explicitly as a proof of what a fully-enabled island could achieve, not a finished answer to Shetland's capacity problem.

That distinction matters, because a few years later, Shetland's *actual* long-term answer arrived, and it looked nothing like NINES.

In 2019, Ofgem and SSEN Transmission commissioned Baringa to run a formal options appraisal for Shetland's electricity future out to 2042. Three options went into that appraisal: a distribution-level subsea cable with backup generation (£452.6m), a new on-island thermal power station (£620.7m), and a high-voltage DC transmission link to the mainland. The HVDC link won, and Ofgem ultimately approved a capital cost allowance of £641.8m for it — one of the largest single network investments of its kind in the UK.

The word "NINES" does not appear anywhere in that appraisal. Nor does it appear in SSEN's companion Long-Term Strategy document for Shetland. The only mention of demand management in either document is a single footnote, noting that active network management to constrain demand might be needed as a *supporting* measure once an infrastructure option was chosen — not as an alternative to be weighed against one.

It would be easy to read this as an indictment: a network operator spent £35m proving a concept, then ignored it when the real money was on the table. Having dug further, I don't think that's quite right — and the actual explanation turns out to be more interesting than an oversight.

While Baringa's options appraisal was being written, something else was happening on Shetland that had nothing to do with local demand at all. A large onshore wind farm, Viking Energy, needed a way to export its power to the mainland grid before its Contracts for Difference subsidy window closed — Viking was pushing Ofgem for a decision on cost-sharing months ahead of a hard Final Investment Decision deadline in March 2020. Viking itself described its project, in a submission to Ofgem, as the "anchor project" for the interconnector, consuming a large share of its 600MW capacity. Ofgem's own later decision confirming the project's need states plainly that without the interconnector, Viking "would not be able to safely connect to the National Electricity Transmission System due to the lack of transmission capacity."

The Baringa appraisal document itself doesn't mention Viking by name, and frames its own purpose around the same local demand and security-of-supply question NINES was built to answer. But the project that actually got approved — sized at 600MW, driven by a subsidy-auction deadline — looks like a decision substantially reshaped by a much bigger, unrelated problem that arrived while the appraisal was underway. Once exporting several hundred megawatts of new wind generation became the priority, the original housing/demand-side question this whole comparison turns on most likely became beside the point for this particular £641.8m decision — not rejected, not out-competed, just quietly superseded. That's my own reading connecting two separate regulatory documents, not something either one states outright, so I'd hold it as the most likely explanation rather than a confirmed one.

What doesn't change, whichever explanation is right: nobody has published a version of this decision that puts a demand-side alternative on the same page as the option that was chosen. A reader relying only on the headline appraisal document would have no way to know Viking's export deadline was doing most of the work — that context lives in separate filings most people would never think to check. Whole-system decisions this size are better served by saying, in one place, what problem is actually being solved and why an alternative wasn't in scope, rather than requiring anyone curious to reconstruct it from four different regulatory documents, the way this piece had to.

There's also a second reason worth naming, independent of Viking, and it has nothing to do with oversight. Even setting aside what the interconnector was really for, NINES and any future demand-side option would still have struggled to be compared on the interconnector's own terms, because the two were never speaking the same language. NINES's economics report measures cost-effectiveness in pounds per megawatt-hour of curtailment avoided — an energy metric. The interconnector, like almost every transmission investment, is costed in pounds per megawatt of capacity delivered — a capacity metric. You cannot rank a £/MWh number against a £/MW number any more than you can compare a fuel price in pence per litre against a road toll in pence per mile. NINES's own battery achieved a curtailment-mitigation cost of around £203 per MWh, against a theoretical best case of £278 — genuinely useful numbers, on their own terms. But there is no clean way to convert that into pounds per kilowatt of capacity deferred, which is the only number that would let you put NINES and the interconnector on the same page. As far as the public record shows, nobody built that conversion. Demand-side didn't lose on cost. It was never measured on a scale that made a contest possible.

There's a related finding worth naming here too, independent of cost. NINES's own Project Close-Down Report is candid about why the domestic demand-management layer specifically struggled, in terms that have nothing to do with battery technology: "the speed of recruitment of DSM in a domestic and commercial market place is extremely slow in comparison to conventional reinforcement," and "the underlying fixed costs (in particular communications) make small scale demand side management... uneconomic," with net communications and support costs running £330–380 per house per year — a fixed overhead the report says needs "an order of magnitude reduction" to become viable. That is a genuinely separate finding from the units-mismatch point above: even setting aside how NINES was priced against the interconnector, its own evidence shows small-scale domestic DSM was slow and expensive to run, on its own terms, regardless of storage cost.

This is not simply a historical curiosity, either. In 2025, SSEN published a Strategic Development Plan for Shetland's Gremista grid supply point — a lighter-weight, earlier-stage planning document than the Baringa appraisal, but the same operator, the same island, six years on. It identifies secondary transformers projected to overload by 2028, and it states plainly that "flexibility at a local level... is likely to be required alongside traditional reinforcement." It even acknowledges, using SSEN's own vulnerability scenarios, that the households least able to afford new heating technology may also be least able to provide the flexibility being asked of them. And once again, flexibility appears as a supporting measure, not as a costed alternative to reinforcement. The pattern that produced the 2019 outcome hasn't been fixed. It's being repeated.

If there's a case for optimism in this story, it needs to be stated carefully, because it is easy to overstate. NINES's battery was lead-acid technology, specified in 2013 and installed by 2016; grid-scale battery prices generally have fallen by more than 90% in real terms since 2010, and the chemistry NINES used has been overtaken by lithium iron phosphate, offering several times the cycle life at a fraction of the historical cost. But the binding constraint NINES actually exposed was never battery cost — it was the cost and difficulty of recruiting and coordinating domestic flexibility at scale, and a fixed communications overhead that doesn't shrink with the number of participating homes. Cheaper batteries don't fix that, and it would be a mistake to read this case as "if only storage had been cheaper, NINES would have worked." It wasn't storage that struggled; NINES's own battery performed reasonably well on its own terms. What struggled was the domestic demand-side management layer built around it.

What the battery-price collapse does change is which comparison is worth running now — and it's worth actually doing the arithmetic rather than just gesturing at "batteries got cheaper." NINES's battery cost £3,974,000 for 3MWh: £1,325 per kWh installed. NREL's 2025 cost-projection report puts a modern utility-scale 4-hour lithium-ion system's installed cost at $334/kWh (roughly £263) — a defensible, bottom-up modelled figure; Ember's October 2025 analysis of actual global auction outcomes outside China and the US puts it lower still, at $125/kWh (roughly £98), reflecting real recent procurement rather than a model. Even applying a substantial premium for a remote Shetland site on top of the more conservative NREL figure, the same £3.97m would plausibly buy somewhere between 2.5 and 5 times NINES's original 3MWh today; using the more aggressive Ember figure, the multiple could run into the 7-to-13x range. If curtailment avoided scaled in rough proportion to capacity, NINES's own £203/MWh curtailment-mitigation cost could fall to somewhere in the £15–80/MWh range depending which figure you use — potentially inside wholesale power price territory. That calculation is deliberately naive (it assumes the original battery's opportunity was capacity-limited, not opportunity-limited, and no one has done the real system-design work to confirm it), but the direction is clear enough to state plainly. NINES, as deployed, bundled active network management, a grid-scale battery, and domestic DSM into one trial, and the DSM component turned out to be the expensive, slow part; the ANM system's own curtailment benefit (1.2GWh/year) was already roughly 11 times domestic DSM's contribution (105.7 MWh/year), on 2013-era hardware. A version of that trial built today around active network management and modern LFP storage — without the domestic DSM layer that proved so difficult to scale — is scaling up the part that already worked, at a fraction of the cost. That is the live question this history should raise, not a retroactive verdict on the 2019 decision: an appraisal is judged on what it considered at the time, not on what got cheaper afterwards. But it does mean the continued absence of that comparison is harder to justify now than it would have been in 2013, not easier.

And the demand-side option is no longer hypothetical, even locally. In Dumfries, a company called Kight PowerHub is opening Scotland's first domestic battery-storage factory, developed over six years with South of Scotland Enterprise and designed with direct input from social housing landlords. Units are already installed in South of Scotland social housing, with the company reporting — its own figure, not yet independently audited — an average 80% reduction in tenant electricity bills. Whatever the final, verified number turns out to be, the fact of a Scottish factory building purpose-designed domestic storage for social housing tenants is itself evidence that the negawatt side of this argument has moved from a trial on a windswept island to a manufacturing line.

Here's the part that turns this from a historical curiosity into something worth real attention now. The interconnector Viking needed didn't actually solve Viking's problem. Independent analysis from the UK Energy Research Centre puts Viking's curtailment at roughly 60% across its first couple of years of operation — not because the Shetland-to-mainland link is too small, but because the wider bottleneck moving power from Scotland to England is full. The Viking-specific numbers are stark on their own: SSE received over £2.1m in constraint payments for Viking in August 2024 alone; across 2025, Viking discarded 1.1 million MWh of generation — 27% of all of Scotland's wasted onshore wind energy — earning £9.6m in constraint payments; cumulative payments since Viking came online in August 2023 have reached roughly £19m. SSE itself has been explicit about the cause: "the UK has built renewables where the resources are strongest, and now we need to upgrade the grid to eliminate bottlenecks." Viking sits alongside two other flagship Scottish wind farms, Seagreen and Moray East, similarly throttled. Across Great Britain, the resulting constraint payments — money paid to generators not to generate, funded through consumer bills — totalled £1.65 billion in just fifteen months, from January 2024 to April 2025.

It would be tempting to frame this as "storage versus transmission" — the same question NINES asked, just at national scale. That framing doesn't survive contact with the actual physics. Viking's curtailment is caused by a fully congested transmission boundary, not a lack of local demand or storage. A battery sited next to Viking cannot export power past a boundary that's already full, any more than the wind farm itself can — storage doesn't add transmission capacity. What it can do is time-shift: absorb Viking's output during the hours the boundary is congested, then either serve local demand or export later when there's headroom. That's a harder, more specific question than "soak up curtailment locally," and as far as the public record shows, nobody has published a costed answer to it — at Viking's scale or Britain's.

That is precisely the shape of problem NINES was built, in miniature, to test a version of: flexible, timed demand or storage absorbing renewable output the network can't otherwise carry. NINES asked its version of that question at Shetland's scale, for £35m, and never got a fair hearing against the £600m answer that was chosen instead for an unrelated reason. The sharper version of the same question, asked today at the scale of Britain's curtailment bill, is worth over a billion pounds a year — and still doesn't have a published, costed answer.

One more gap is worth naming honestly, because it cuts against the trial rather than for it. NINES altered the heating systems of several hundred households on an island with a documented fuel poverty rate of 40% — one of the highest of any Scottish local authority, against a 31% national average (Scottish Government, 2022–2024) — and 1,854 people there received emergency fuel vouchers in under three years. Nothing in NINES's economics, ANM, or battery reports measures whether those households ended up warmer or paid less to heat their homes. That's not a knock on what NINES was built to do, which was a grid-capacity question, not a welfare one. But SSEN's current strategy for Shetland names fuel poverty as its top priority, and doesn't mention NINES anywhere. If a similar trial were designed today, on an island this fuel-poor, tenant outcomes seem like something worth measuring alongside curtailment — a second question this history leaves open, distinct from the first.

The lesson from Shetland isn't that demand-side flexibility beats network reinforcement, or that it doesn't. It's that nobody has yet built a comparison capable of answering that question — not in 2019, when the trial and the decision happened in the same place within a few years of each other, and not now, when the same question is costing the country over a billion pounds a year. Until that comparison exists — a genuine cost-per-kilowatt-of-capacity-deferred figure for demand-side and storage options, priced the same way network reinforcement is priced — every year of uncosted curtailment is the same gap from 2019, just with a much bigger price tag attached.
