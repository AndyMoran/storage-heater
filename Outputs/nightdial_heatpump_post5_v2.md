Heat pumps cut carbon by 77% vs gas. Add the right tariff and they're nearly a third cheaper to run. The blocker isn't physics. It's policy.

Across 40 real households, the average winter heat demand is 4,720 kWh. Delivering that heat three different ways gives three very different carbon outcomes:

- Gas: 958 kg CO₂/year
- Storage heating (real usage): 678 kg — a 29% cut
- Storage heating (perfectly run): 621 kg
- Heat pump (real field SCOP 2.78): 223 kg — a 77% cut, and two-thirds lower than even storage heating's best case

These figures use real, demand-weighted 2026 carbon intensities, matched night-by-night to the temperatures that actually drive heat demand. No cherry-picking, no manufacturer numbers — just the real grid, real weather, and real households.

Storage heating genuinely beats gas, and more consistently than I first expected. But heat pumps are in a different league: across all 40 households, not one comes close to gas on a demand-weighted basis.

On cost, the numbers cut both ways: at today's prices, a heat pump on a standard tariff costs £470 a year to run — more than gas's £418. Put the same heat pump on a heat-pump-specific tariff (Economy 7 or Octopus Cosy) and it drops to £302 — nearly a third cheaper than gas. Same hardware, same house, same SCOP. The tariff is the whole difference.

Which is the real problem. The barrier isn't physics — it's price design, installer capacity, funding rules that exclude social housing, and tenure constraints. All policy choices, not laws of nature.
