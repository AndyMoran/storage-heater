# The rational choice is the dangerous one

There's a framing I've used carelessly in earlier posts from this project, and I want to correct it here: when a tenant switches off a storage heater and heats themselves with a plug-in radiator instead, that is not irrational behaviour born of desperation or poor judgement. Checked against real numbers, it's very nearly the opposite. It's the correct answer to the question they were actually asked.

## The comparison nobody puts in one table

I built the full running-cost comparison for a typical two-bedroom social housing flat, using real current prices, for every heating approach this project has studied so far.

**[Chart here: fig6_rational_point_heating.png]**

Ranked from cheapest to most expensive:

| Approach | Annual cost |
|---|---|
| Plug-in radiator + electric blanket | £280 |
| Gas central heating | £378 |
| Storage heater, fully optimised (smart timing, zero waste) | £691 |
| Storage heater, unmanaged (today's typical behaviour) | £927 |

Read that top row again. Heating one room with a plug-in electric radiator, and yourself overnight with an electric blanket, costs *less than gas central heating* — and dramatically less than even the best version of the smart storage-heater system I've spent months building. Not close to competitive. Genuinely, measurably cheaper than everything else on the list.

If you are a tenant with a fixed income and a bill you're struggling to pay, and your only goal is spending less money on heat, that top row is the correct answer. Not a corner-cutting compromise. The actual optimal solution to the problem you've been handed.

## What the table doesn't show

Point heating works by warming a person, not a home. The room around them stays close to outdoor temperature. Cold surfaces — walls, window reveals, the corners behind furniture — condense the moisture that ordinary living produces: cooking, breathing, washing, drying clothes indoors. Condensation, sustained, becomes damp. Damp, sustained, becomes mould.

This isn't a chain of assumptions I've built to make a point. There's real evidence behind every link.

A peer-reviewed study using actual GB smart-meter data on 11,500 prepayment customers found something genuinely counter-intuitive: households self-disconnect from their supply *more* during cold periods than at other times — despite needing heat the most, precisely because that's when the cost of keeping the heating on is highest relative to what they have to spend. Separately, recent survey data on prepayment customers is blunter still: over half have drastically cut their energy use in recent months, and more than a third now describe their home as cold and damp as a direct result of that choice.

That's the mechanism, measured, not inferred: unaffordable heating leads to reduced heating leads to damp leads to mould. Every link in that chain now has a number behind it.

## Where Awaab's Law changes who's actually exposed

Awaab's Law — introduced under the Social Housing (Regulation) Act 2023, named for a two-year-old who died from prolonged mould exposure in a housing association property — puts a fixed statutory clock on social landlords: investigate reported damp and mould hazards, and fix them, within defined timeframes. Miss it, and there's real regulatory and reputational exposure, not just a strongly worded complaint.

Put the two findings together and something uncomfortable falls out. The tenant's rational, cost-minimising response to an unaffordable heating system — use less of it — is the exact behaviour that produces the damp and mould their landlord is now legally obligated to fix. The tenant did nothing wrong. They solved the problem in front of them correctly. The landlord inherits the consequence of a heating system whose running costs made that the rational choice in the first place.

I don't think that reframing is just semantic. It changes where the actual fix has to live. You cannot educate someone out of a decision that was already correct. Telling a tenant who is already paying £280 instead of £927 to "manage their heating better" isn't advice — it's asking them to spend money they don't have to solve a problem they've already solved for themselves, just not in the way that keeps their home dry.

## What this means for what gets built

Two things change the incentive, and only two, as far as this data shows. Make the whole-home heating option cheaper — which is exactly what the smart timing and overcharge-elimination work in this project targets, closing part of the gap between £927 and £280 rather than lecturing anyone about it. Or reduce how much heat is needed in the first place — proper insulation, which this project's own numbers show can cut the underlying demand by 35–50%, shrinking every figure in that table at once, point-heating included.

Neither of those is a communication problem. Both are engineering and policy problems. That's the distinction I think is worth sitting with before assuming a nudge, a leaflet, or a friendlier app is what's actually missing here.
