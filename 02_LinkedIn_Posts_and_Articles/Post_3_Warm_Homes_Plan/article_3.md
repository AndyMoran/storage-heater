# Does the Warm Homes Plan already solve the problem I've been measuring?

Since I started publishing real numbers from this project, the policy landscape hasn't stood still. In January the government announced the Warm Homes Plan — £15 billion, the biggest home energy upgrade programme in UK history — and one line in the coverage stopped me: households with electric storage heating are expected to save £442 a year from April 2026.

That's bigger than any number I've published myself. So before writing anything else about this project, I owed it an honest answer to an uncomfortable question: has the government just made this whole exercise redundant?

I checked properly rather than take the headline at face value — the same discipline I've tried to apply to every number in this project so far.

## What £442 actually is

It isn't a smarter-heating saving. It's a price cut, and a fairly specific one.

**[Chart 1 here: fig5_warmhomes_breakdown.png, left panel]**

The government's own detailed calculation puts the underlying figure at £154 for a typical household, made up of three concrete policy moves: roughly £88 from shifting Renewables Obligation costs off energy bills and onto general taxation, £59 from ending the ECO energy efficiency scheme's bill-funded model, and £7 from the associated VAT effect. All three reduce the price of a unit of electricity. None of them change when or how much electricity anyone actually uses.

Storage-heated households get £442 instead of the £150 "average" for one simple reason: they use a lot more electricity than a typical home, because they're heating an entire house electrically. The same price-per-unit cut, applied to a much bigger number of units, produces a much bigger pound figure. That's arithmetic on consumption volume — it says nothing about whether that consumption is well-timed or wasteful.

## Why that means it's additive, not competing

Everything I've published in this project measures a completely different thing: not the price of electricity, but whether a household is buying more of it than the weather actually justifies, and whether it's timed to avoid the most expensive hours.

**[Chart 2 here: fig5_warmhomes_breakdown.png, right panel]**

Those two mechanisms don't overlap. A household gets the government's price cut automatically, through their tariff, regardless of how they use their heater. Separately, fixing the actual overcharging — what this project has spent months quantifying — is worth £236 a year on top of that, for a household running the level of unmanaged waste I've measured in real data. Stack them together and you get £678, not a smaller number because one somehow absorbed the other.

The Warm Homes Plan is a real, large, welcome intervention. It just isn't the intervention I've been building. Worth being precise about that distinction rather than letting a bigger headline number quietly make a smaller, different one seem redundant.

## How big is this problem, actually?

While I was untangling that, I went looking for a number I'd been assuming rather than checking: how many homes does this actually affect?

Real, sourced figures: roughly 1.7 million homes across the UK are still heated by electric storage heaters. The English Housing Survey's own data shows about a quarter of storage-heated homes have automatic charge control — meaning roughly three-quarters, an estimated 1.26 million homes nationally, are still running the old manual dial system this project is built around.

Narrow that to the population this project actually targets — housing associations — and the number is more specific: 182,000 to 187,000 homes in housing association stock are primarily storage-heated. Applying the same manual-control ratio puts the addressable figure at roughly **136,500 homes** still on old-style manual dials, within social housing alone.

That's not a niche problem, and it's not just my own framing of it. National Energy Action — a fuel poverty charity with no stake in whether this project succeeds — states plainly that outdated, inefficient electric storage heaters are trapping hundreds of thousands of social housing tenants in fuel poverty right now. That's independent corroboration of the exact mechanism this project has spent months quantifying from smart-meter data, arrived at from a completely different direction.

## Where this leaves things

A real government policy just delivered a genuinely large saving to exactly the population I've been studying — and checking it properly showed it solves a different problem, not this one. The scale of what's left unaddressed is over a hundred thousand homes in social housing alone, with an independent fuel-poverty charity already flagging the harm.

I think that combination is worth documenting clearly before moving further into the hardware and app work — not because the numbers are more exciting than what I published before, but because they're the ones that actually justify finishing this properly rather than assuming a bigger headline already covers it.

Still building this in the open. Still looking for people who know this space better than I do.
