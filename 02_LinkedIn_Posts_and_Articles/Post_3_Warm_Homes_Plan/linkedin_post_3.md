Quick one: the government just announced storage-heated households will save £442/year from April. Does that mean the problem I've been measuring is already solved?

I checked properly rather than assume either way. Short answer: no — and the reason why is worth knowing if you work anywhere near social housing or energy policy.

That £442 figure isn't a smarter-heating saving. It's a price cut. The government is moving £154 worth of green levies (Renewables Obligation costs, the ending ECO scheme, VAT) off electricity bills and onto general taxation instead. Storage-heated households get a bigger number than the £150 "average" simply because they use more electricity overall — same price cut, bigger bill, bigger pound saving. It has nothing to do with when or how efficiently anyone actually uses their heater.

Which means it's not competing with what I've been building. It's additive. The government's price cut and a genuine timing/waste fix stack on top of each other:

→ Warm Homes Plan (price cut): £442
→ Fixing the actual overcharging (what my project measures): £236
→ Both together: £678

While I was checking that, I also went looking for how big this problem actually is. Real numbers: roughly 1.7 million UK homes still run on electric storage heating. Using the English Housing Survey's own split between manual dial-controlled and automatic-charge-control heaters, that's an estimated 1.26 million homes still on the old manual system nationally — and specifically within housing associations, around 136,500 homes.

That's not a rounding error. And it's not just my read on it — National Energy Action, an actual fuel poverty charity with no stake in my project existing, states plainly that outdated storage heaters are trapping hundreds of thousands of social housing tenants in fuel poverty right now.

So: real problem, real scale, not solved by the policy everyone's currently reading headlines about. That combination is exactly why I think this is worth finishing properly rather than assuming someone else's number already covers it.
