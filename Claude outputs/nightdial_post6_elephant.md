The lowest-carbon heating strategy in this entire project isn't a heat pump. It's a single electric bar heater and an electric blanket, used by someone who can't afford anything else.

I want to state that plainly, because it's the uncomfortable result sitting underneath everything else I've published this month, and I think it deserves its own post rather than a footnote.

Across the real households and prices I've been using throughout this project: point heating and an electric blanket runs under £300 a year — cheaper than gas, cheaper than a heat pump, cheaper than storage heating by a wide margin. It also uses the least electricity of any option on the table, which means it has the smallest carbon footprint of anything I've measured. By the numbers alone, it looks like the responsible choice.

It isn't. It's what's left when someone genuinely can't afford to heat their whole home, and it comes with a bill that never appears on their energy statement. Cold homes cost the NHS £540 million a year in England alone; poor housing more broadly — cold, damp, and the mould that follows — costs £1.4 billion a year in first-year treatment costs, before counting the damage to the housing stock itself or a lifetime of chronic health effects. Children carry a disproportionate share of that risk. Awaab Ishak was two years old when he died from mould exposure in a Rochdale housing association flat in 2020. The law that followed his death — requiring social landlords to treat excess cold as a legal hazard, not a lifestyle choice — comes into force this November.

And here's the part that made me want to write this post: the technology this population is actually stuck with, storage heating, is one of the most expensive ways to heat a home I've found anywhere in this project — more expensive than gas, more expensive than a heat pump on almost any tariff, £964 a year even run badly and still £719 a year run perfectly. So the person with the least money is choosing between the priciest heating system on the table and rationing themselves down to a point heater and a blanket. Both routes are rational, given what they're actually facing. Neither is a solution.

This is the part every clean carbon or cost chart I've built this month quietly leaves out. A spreadsheet comparing kg of CO2 or pounds spent will always rank the coldest household as the most efficient one. That's not a flaw in the arithmetic — the arithmetic is right. It's a sign the arithmetic is answering the wrong question. Nobody should have to be sick to be counted as green.

None of this is a call for sympathy over data — this project has tried to be scrupulous about that throughout. It's a call to notice what the data is actually telling us: the people bearing the smallest carbon footprint in this whole comparison are bearing the largest cost for it, in cold, in health, and in a heating system that was never going to be affordable in the first place. That's not a personal failing. It's what happens when the cheapest technology to install is also the most expensive to run, and nobody redesigned the market to notice.
