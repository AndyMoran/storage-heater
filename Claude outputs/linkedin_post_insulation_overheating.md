**Insulation doesn't keep summer heat out any better than it keeps winter heat in. That's the problem.**

The UK government's own July 2026 study of reversible air-to-air heat pumps modelled five home archetypes for heating and cooling performance. The result that jumped out: the newest, most-insulated home in the study — a Future Homes Standard new-build — needed active cooling far more than any of the older, smaller archetypes tested. Living room temperatures crept above 25°C for 8% of a typical year, rising to 15% in a heatwave scenario. The older housing stock in the same study barely needed cooling at all — under 1% of the year.

It's tempting to read that as "insulation causes overheating." It doesn't, and that distinction actually matters more than the headline.

Insulation is built to retain heat — that's the whole point of it in winter. But it doesn't know which direction the heat is trying to go. A Welsh Government factsheet on this exact problem puts it plainly: insulation "tends to further contribute to overheating" once heat has already got in, because it removes one of the accidental escape routes that older, leakier homes had. The actual causes of overheating are airtightness without enough ventilation, large south-facing glazing, and not enough shading. Insulation just means that once you've let the heat in, it's staying.

Which is why a highly insulated home doesn't have to overheat at all — it just needs the other half of the equation. Shade the glazing. Get ventilation right, especially purge ventilation at night. Use thermal mass and site conditions if you've got them. Do that, and insulation is doing exactly what it's meant to: keeping conditioned air where you want it, in both directions.

The uncomfortable part for UK housing policy: current Building Regulations and the Future Homes Standard are optimised hard for winter heat loss, and DESNZ's own modelling shows the summer side of that trade-off isn't being caught. As newer, tighter homes become the norm, the gap between "well insulated" and "well designed for a warming climate" is exactly where our best-insulated stock is quietly landing in a cooling-demand conversation that official UK grid planning (NESO's Future Energy Scenarios, DESNZ's own study here) still doesn't model the electricity-system consequences of.

We've spent two decades building for the last problem. Time to design for the one that's actually here.
