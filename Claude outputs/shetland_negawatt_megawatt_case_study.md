# Negawatt vs. Megawatt: A Shetland Case Study

*Working research note — Heaviside Analytics*

## 1. Origin and framing

This note grew out of a wider question in the storage-heater/social-housing VPP series: as winter electrification of heat accelerates, is anyone systematically comparing the cost of upgrading housing (reducing peak heat demand — a "negawatt") against the cost of upgrading the grid to carry that demand (a "megawatt")? An initial attempt to test this through a demographic lens — do more deprived areas show a sharper, more threshold-like demand response to cold weather — was scoped back after two problems surfaced: the mechanism most likely to distinguish behavioural rationing from simple building-fabric effects (prepayment credit depletion tied to income-payment cycles) needs household-level data that isn't realistically accessible outside SERL's Secure Lab accreditation route, and the resulting output would have been diagnostic ("here's a correlation") rather than something a DNO, regulator, or prospective consultancy client could act on.

This note pursues the more directly useful version of the same underlying question: find a real, costed instance where a grid operator faced a demand-side (negawatt) alternative to a supply/network-side (megawatt) investment, and check whether that comparison was actually made.

**How this note is structured.** It does five distinct things, in order, and keeps them clearly separated because they carry different kinds of evidence: first, it documents a specific historical finding — what actually happened on Shetland, and what wasn't compared to what, and why (sections 2–6); second, it asks a counterfactual — given how far battery technology has moved since NINES was specified, how would that historical comparison look if re-run today (section 7); third, it sets out present-day parallels — live, ongoing cases showing the same underlying pattern (or its resolution) happening now, elsewhere or in the same place (section 8); fourth, it argues the underlying question is no longer a Shetland-specific or historical one at all, but a live, national-scale problem worth well over a billion pounds a year (section 9); fifth, it names a dimension the original trial's own evaluation never measured at all — fuel poverty — using Shetland's current, documented fuel poverty rate rather than NINES-era data (section 10). The historical finding stands on its own regardless of what follows; sections 7-10 are there to show the finding isn't a closed historical curiosity.

## 2. Why Shetland

Shetland is close to the best-case location in the UK for this test. It has:

- A decade-old, well-funded, well-documented smart demand-management trial (NINES) run by the incumbent network operator, specifically targeting electric storage and water heating load.
- A subsequent, very large, independently-appraised capital investment decision (a new subsea transmission link) to solve the same underlying problem — insufficient local capacity — made by the same operator group.
- A public, though partially redacted, paper trail covering both.

If a demand-side option was ever going to show up in a formal UK grid investment appraisal, Shetland is where you'd expect to find it.

## 3. NINES: the demand-side precedent

Northern Isles New Energy Solutions (NINES) was an SSEN-led trial funded through Ofgem's Low Carbon Networks Fund (LCNF Tier 2).

- **Total project cost:** £34.95m.
- **Scope:** installation of smart domestic space and water heating control into an initial 750 homes, with an option to extend to a further 250 (i.e. up to c. 1,000 homes) — the same population later referred to elsewhere as "smart storage heater replacement in 1,000 homes."
- **Storage:** a 1MW/3MWh battery installed at Lerwick Power Station for energy balancing and frequency response — reportedly Europe's largest lead-acid installation at the time. **Correction:** earlier drafts of this note described this as a sodium-sulphur (NaS) battery. A 2017 conference talk by Stewart Reid (SSEN Head of Innovation, IET.tv) states the project originally specified NaS technology but switched to lead-acid (VRLA) before installation after safety concerns with NaS at the time of procurement — consistent with the "VRLA" chemistry given in the 2A Battery Operational Effectiveness report (see section 11).
- **Aim:** one of several trialled "alternative solutions, including demand side response and energy storage, that if successful could be implemented to reduce the overall cost of meeting the electricity needs of Shetland" — specifically targeted at reducing the capacity required from a *replacement local power station*, by a modest 7MW.

**How recruitment and cost actually played out.** NINES's own Project Close-Down Report is candid that the demand-side layer, specifically, was the hard part operationally — independent of battery technology or curtailment economics. Recruitment fell well short of plan, from 750 homes targeted to 234 actually enrolled, after Shetland Islands Council withdrew from the scheme in 2012, and the report states plainly: "The speed of recruitment of DSM in a domestic and commercial market place is extremely slow in comparison to conventional reinforcement and asset related smart solutions." It identifies the underlying economic problem just as directly: "The underlying fixed costs (in particular communications) make small scale demand side management (Small pockets of demand in a high number of locations) uneconomic" — putting the net communications and support cost at £330–£380 per house per year, and concluding that "becoming viable requires an order of magnitude reduction in the communications costs, and/or additional subsidy payments." This is a distinct finding from the £/MWh-vs-£/MW measurement mismatch discussed in section 11 — it's about the practical, operational cost of running domestic DSM at small scale, not about how that cost was (or wasn't) priced against the interconnector.

**Caveat:** £34.95m divided across 750–1,000 homes is roughly £35,000–£47,000 per home. This is the cost of the whole R&D trial — monitoring infrastructure, the battery, evaluation — not a clean per-home retrofit or control-unit cost, and should never be quoted as if it were.

**A cleaner, present-day per-home benchmark exists for the "negawatt" side.** Connected Response's HeatSage retrofit control (a load switch plus temperature/humidity sensor added to existing electric storage heaters, recognised as a SAP-compliant measure) has real, publicly-reported deployment costs from 2026 Scottish social housing projects: South Lanarkshire Council (Cambuslang), £70,000 across 144 flats (≈£486/home); Trust Housing Association (Angus), £45,118 across 58 properties (≈£778/home); West Dunbartonshire, £355,600 across 900 homes (≈£395/home). This gives a genuine ~£400–£780-per-home range for demand-side control retrofit, a useful contrast against the Eildon £5,000-per-home heat pump capital figure below, or the £7.5m Maiden Hill substation cost. A separately-reported "30% average energy reduction" claim for HeatSage could not be independently confirmed and is not used here.

## 4. The interconnector: the eventual supply-side answer

Years later, Shetland's long-term electricity needs were addressed through a formal options appraisal (Baringa, for Ofgem/SSEN Transmission, published 2019) comparing three supply-side options over a 20-year horizon (2023–2042):

| Option | Description | Estimated cost (2023–42) |
|---|---|---|
| D-link | Distribution-level subsea cable + backup generation | £452.6m |
| On-island generation | New thermal power station | £620.7m |
| T-link | HVDC transmission link to the mainland | ≈£371.2m (chosen option) |

Ofgem subsequently approved a total capital cost allowance of **£641.8m** for the Shetland HVDC interconnector and Kergord converter station (trimmed from SSEN-Transmission's £675.4m request, largely through reduced risk allowances and disallowing certain specific costs).

**The interconnector's purpose shifted while it was being appraised, and this matters for section 5.** Ofgem's own Decision on the Final Needs Case (July 2020) states there is "clear technical need for the reinforcement" because, without it, "[the Viking Energy Wind Farm] and other generation projects would not be able to safely connect to the National Electricity Transmission System due to the lack of transmission capacity." Viking Energy Wind Farm — a large onshore wind farm on Shetland, reported at 457MW in a July 2019 Ofgem filing and at 370MW in later retrospective coverage (an unreconciled discrepancy, likely reflecting a design change over the intervening years, not resolved here) — describes itself in that same 2019 filing as the "anchor project" for the interconnector, consuming a large share of its 600MW capacity. Viking was working to a hard deadline: it needed Ofgem's cost-contribution methodology confirmed before the 2019 Contracts for Difference auction closed, in order to reach Final Investment Decision by March 2020.

The Baringa options-appraisal document itself (the one comparing D-link/on-island generation/T-link) does not mention Viking by name, and frames its own purpose as serving "both island demand and wind generation capacity," with local demand and security of supply — driven by Lerwick Power Station's planned closure — described as the foundational driver; wind export is framed as an opportunity that became live only after a 2017 rule change let island wind farms enter the CfD auction. So the most defensible reading, bridging both documents, is this: the appraisal that compared the three supply-side options started out addressing broadly the same territory as NINES (local demand and security of supply), but the project that resulted from it was sized, timed, and ultimately justified substantially by the need to export a several-hundred-megawatt wind farm — something no amount of local demand-side flexibility could substitute for. This is this note's own inference connecting two separate documents, not a stated fact in either one.

**Clarifying the £371.2m T-link figure and its relationship to the DSO contribution.** These are two separate cost adjustments, not one net figure — an earlier version of this note conflated them. Ofgem's May 2019 SHEPD contribution consultation, directly quoted, confirms two things precisely: "For Shetland: SHE-T's estimate is £709m," and separately, "our initial benchmarking exercise indicates that the capital costs for the Shetland project could be significantly lower – in the range of £368m to £395m." £709m − the £251m DSO contribution = £458m, not £371.2m, so the £371.2m figure in the table above is not "the £709m baseline net of the DSO contribution." £371.2m does sit just below Ofgem's own £368m–£395m benchmarked range, which is suggestive — but that exact figure does not itself appear anywhere in this consultation document, and the £371.2m figure in this note's table originates from the later (2019) Baringa options appraisal, a separate document from the one quoted here. So the link between "£371.2m" and "Ofgem's £368-395m benchmarking" is this note's own inference bridging two different documents, not a fact stated in either source — flagged as such rather than presented as confirmed. The DSO contribution itself is a distinct, well-documented mechanism, confirmed directly from Ofgem's December 2019 SHEPD contribution decision: £251m, split into Capacity Support (£123m, reflecting the transmission cable's role in securing demand supply after Lerwick Power Station's planned decommissioning), Control Support (£117.5m, for network-stability benefits from replacing on-island generation with HVDC converter capability), and Losses Reduction (£10.2m). The cost was then shared between connecting generators and the wider consumer base: part reduced local circuit charges via an amendment to CUSC paragraph 14.15.75, with the remainder recovered through wider TNUoS charges under CUSC paragraph 14.15.76 — spreading the cost across all GB transmission users rather than concentrating it on Shetland's island generators. This is a concrete example of the regulatory framework already being flexible enough to share network costs across a "whole system" boundary — which sharpens, rather than undermines, this note's core finding: the framework could evidently accommodate novel cost-sharing arrangements, yet no demand-side alternative was brought into the same appraisal.

## 5. The key finding: no demand-side option in the formal comparison

Neither the Baringa appraisal report nor SSEN's own Long-Term Strategy for Shetland document evaluates a demand-side or efficiency-based option as an alternative to new supply/transmission capacity. The word "NINES" does not appear in either document. The only reference to demand management in the Long-Term Strategy is a single footnote noting that Active Network Management "to constrain demand" might be needed as a *supporting* measure alongside whichever infrastructure option is chosen — not as a considered alternative to it.

**What this shows, revised in light of section 4's Viking Energy finding:** NINES was run as a genuine test of whether demand-side management could affect the island's grid-capacity constraint — that was its explicit purpose. But by the time the formal, multi-hundred-million-pound options appraisal was produced, the underlying question had changed shape. NINES was sized to a modest local problem (helping absorb more renewable generation into island demand, targeting roughly 7-14MW). The interconnector, once Viking Energy Wind Farm's several-hundred-megawatt export need became its anchor justification and primary capacity driver, was answering a much larger question that a local demand-management trial was never going to speak to: how do you get a new industrial-scale wind farm's power off the island at all. Once wind export became the priority, the original demand-side/housing-capacity question this project cares about most likely became beside the point for that specific £641.8m decision — not rejected on the merits, but superseded by a different, larger problem that arrived while the appraisal was in progress. That is the most likely explanation this note has found for NINES's total absence from the published record, though it remains an inference bridging documents rather than a stated rationale in either one (see section 4) — nobody has stated this explanation in so many words, in what's publicly available.

**What is still true regardless of this explanation:** the appraisal was never actually tested against a demand-side alternative, on the record, whatever the reason. A reader relying only on the published options-appraisal and Long-Term Strategy documents would have no way to know that Viking's export need, not local demand, ended up driving the decision — that context sits in separate documents (Ofgem's Needs Case decision, Viking's own submissions) that most readers of the headline appraisal would never see. So the underlying methodological point from earlier drafts of this note still stands even if the moral of the story has softened: whole-system decisions of this size benefit from being explicit, in one place, about which problem they are actually solving and why an alternative wasn't in scope — rather than requiring a reader to reconstruct that from three or four separate regulatory filings, as this note has had to do.

**What this does not show, and should not be claimed:**
- That NINES could have replaced or significantly deferred the interconnector. NINES targeted 7MW of local generation capacity; the interconnector is sized for the whole island's long-term transmission needs — different scales, different problems.
- That the exclusion was an oversight rather than a reasoned scoping decision. The Baringa report is explicitly redacted for publication; an unredacted version may contain scoping rationale that simply isn't public. The fair claim is "the published record shows no consideration of a demand-side option, and no stated reason for its absence" — not "no reason exists."

## 6. Supporting evidence (other UK cases found)

These don't carry the same weight as the Shetland pair, but corroborate that "housing/demand-side vs. grid-side" is a live, recurring tension, not a one-off:

- **West Lothian Council, Mossend/West Calder** — a 69-unit council housing development where air source heat pumps were ruled out due to "insufficient capacity at the substation." No cost figure found for the alternative substation upgrade in public sources checked so far.
- **Maiden Hill, south of Glasgow** — raising a development's low-carbon-technology share from 10% to 30% of properties triggered the need for a new high-voltage substation, costing approximately £7.5m.
- **Dunfermline** — repeated residential developments exhausted available network capacity; further reinforcement estimated at £5–7m, with developers charged £250k per MVA of new capacity until half the infrastructure cost is covered, the remainder socialised.
- **Eildon Housing Association** — estimated heat pump conversion adds at least £5,000 per home in capital cost (a different metric from a general fabric-retrofit cost such as the MEES £10,000-per-measure spend cap used elsewhere in this project; the two should not be conflated).
- **4D Heat (Skye)** — modelled up to £26m/year in consumer benefit and £24m/year in avoided wind-constraint payments from flexible off-gas-grid electric heating, illustrating the wider-system value of demand flexibility even where it isn't the subject of a formal options appraisal.
- **Scale context** — an estimated 380,000 off-gas-grid Scottish homes could plausibly switch to electric heating, potentially absorbing around 540GWh/year of otherwise curtailed wind power by 2030.
- **Sea Link (Suffolk–Kent, 2GW HVDC, live 2026)** — a present-day example of a different kind of appraisal fragility in a major transmission asset. Per Electrical Review (2 September 2026, reporting on an Ofgem proposal — the underlying Ofgem document itself was not independently located and should be treated as secondary-sourced), Ofgem has proposed cutting National Grid's cost allowance from £3.197bn to £2.89155bn (a £305.5m reduction), driven substantially by an unresolved technical issue: even the largest harmonic-filter solutions proposed by bidders were reportedly unlikely to meet Grid Code voltage-waveform-quality limits while the link operates at its full 2GW capacity. This isn't a demand-side-versus-supply-side story like Shetland, but it illustrates the same underlying theme — that large transmission cost appraisals can carry real, late-discovered technical/cost uncertainty — and is worth keeping as a footnote on appraisal fragility rather than a direct parallel.

## 7. What NINES would look like with today's battery technology

NINES's battery — 1MW/3MWh valve-regulated lead-acid (VRLA), £3,974,000 CAPEX, achieving roughly 70% of its planned annual utilisation — was specified against 2013-era technology and installed by 2014-16. It's worth asking what the same trial would look like re-run with a modern lithium iron phosphate (LFP) system, because the answer sharpens rather than weakens this note's core argument.

**Cost — and a unit of measurement caveat that matters.** All the BNEF figures below are *cell/pack* prices ($/kWh of battery cells as manufactured), not *installed system* prices. NINES's £3,974,000 CAPEX, by contrast, was a fully installed system cost — batteries plus enclosures, power conversion, controls, grid connection, and Shetland-specific installation. Pack price is typically only a fraction (commonly cited as roughly half, though this varies by system size and site) of a finished installed system's total cost, so the comparison below shows the direction and scale of the underlying technology shift, not a like-for-like installed-cost prediction for a rebuilt NINES battery. With that caveat: grid-scale lithium-ion pack prices have fallen roughly 90–95% since NINES was specified. BloombergNEF's 2017 survey — contemporaneous with NINES's operational period — put average lithium-ion pack prices at $209/kWh; its 2025 survey puts the *stationary storage* segment specifically at $70/kWh, a 45% drop in that segment alone in just the last year, driven by intense competition and the shift toward LFP chemistry (LFP now averages $81/kWh across all applications, cheaper than the $128/kWh average for NMC/other lithium chemistries). Pack prices overall have fallen from $1,436/kWh in 2010 to $108/kWh in 2025 — better than a 90% real-terms decline over the period spanning NINES's design and today. Even allowing for installed system costs being a multiple of pack price, a same-capacity 1MW/3MWh system built today would cost a fraction of NINES's original £3.97m — or, spent at the same budget, would buy several times the capacity.

**Performance.** LFP also outperforms lead-acid on the two metrics that actually constrained NINES's own results. Cycle life: at a moderate (50%) depth of discharge, LFP typically delivers 6,000–8,000+ cycles against roughly 700–1,200 for lead-acid (AGM/VRLA) — a 5–8x difference — which bears directly on why NINES's battery underperformed its planned 300-cycles/0.9GWh annual utilisation target (2A Battery Operational Effectiveness report, section 4). Round-trip efficiency: LFP systems typically sustain 95%+ efficiency across a wide range of discharge rates, versus a commonly-cited ~80–85% for utility-scale batteries generally in this period (the US EIA measured 82% average monthly round-trip efficiency for utility-scale batteries as of 2019, itself a mixed-chemistry figure, with lead-acid typically at the lower end of that range and prone to greater efficiency loss at higher discharge rates).

**What this does and doesn't fix.** It matters to be precise here, because it's easy to overstate this section into "if only batteries had been cheaper, NINES would have worked." That isn't what the evidence supports. The battery component of NINES performed reasonably on its own terms (£203.14/MWh actual curtailment-mitigation cost, £278.14/MWh theoretical best case) even on decade-old lead-acid technology — it wasn't the part of the trial that struggled. What struggled was the domestic demand-side management layer built around it: recruitment collapsed from a planned 750 homes to 234 after Shetland Islands Council withdrew in 2012 (falling further to 223 by the report's end), and fixed communications-infrastructure costs — 49% of the £3.2m DDSM budget — didn't shrink proportionally as fewer homes joined (see section 3). Cheaper, better-performing batteries do nothing to fix a recruitment and coordination problem, or a fixed-cost communications overhead. NINES did not fail — it proved the active network management concept worked and got 8.5MW of renewables connected — but its own evidence shows domestic DSM specifically was not a cheap or fast route to curtailment relief, independent of storage cost.

**What this means for the argument, not just the technology.** Where the battery-price collapse does matter is in what it changes about which comparison is worth running today. NINES, as deployed, bundled active network management, a grid-scale battery, and domestic DSM into a single trial, and it was the DSM layer that proved expensive and slow to scale — not the storage. A version of that trial built today around active network management plus modern LFP storage, without the domestic DSM component, is a materially different proposition: cheaper per kWh, cycling further before degradation, converting more of every charge into usable discharge (see Performance, above), while the interconnector's £641.8m capital allowance is a sunk, largely fixed cost that doesn't benefit from the same technology curve. This doesn't change the historical finding (no demand-side option was in the formal 2019 appraisal, regardless of what it might have cost), and it is not itself a costed comparison — nobody has published one. But it does mean the continued absence of an ANM-plus-storage comparison, run against reinforcement, is harder to justify today than it would have been when NINES was designed, not easier. That reframing — not "cheaper batteries would have saved NINES," but "cheaper batteries make the missing comparison harder to excuse" — is the more defensible version of this argument, and the present-day parallels in section 8 show the same gap persisting regardless.

**A first-pass quantification (illustrative, not a system design).** NINES's battery cost £3,974,000 ÷ 3,000 kWh = £1,324.67/kWh installed. NREL's 2025 cost-projection report gives a defensible, citable current installed-cost figure for a utility-scale 4-hour LFP system: "$334/kWh" for 2024, derived from a bottom-up, component-by-component model — call it roughly £263/kWh at a $1.27/£ exchange rate. (A separate, lower figure exists — Ember's October 2025 analysis of real-world auction outcomes puts installed cost outside China and the US at $125/kWh, roughly £98/kWh — but that reflects actual recent procurement rather than a modelled estimate, so it's used here as directional confirmation of the same trend, not as the primary number.) Applying a range of remote-site installation premiums (Shetland is not a mainland utility-scale site) to the NREL figure: at no premium, £3.97m buys 15.1MWh of modern storage (5.0x NINES's 3MWh); at a 1.5x premium, 10.1MWh (3.4x); at a 2x premium, 7.6MWh (2.5x). So a defensible range is **roughly 2.5x–5x more storage capacity for the same money** as NINES originally spent.

If curtailment avoided scales in rough proportion to capacity — the same battery doing the same job, just more of it — NINES's own £203.14/MWh curtailment-mitigation cost would fall by a similar factor, landing at **roughly £40–80/MWh** depending on which premium assumption is used. That would put a modern-cost rebuild inside or close to typical wholesale power price territory (tens of £/MWh), a genuinely different economic position than NINES's original £203/MWh, let alone the domestic DSM layer's £5,364.75/MWh.

*This scaling is explicitly naive and should always be presented as such.* It assumes the original battery's curtailment-mitigation opportunity was capacity-limited — that there was more curtailable wind available than 3MWh could absorb, so a bigger battery keeps finding useful work. If the opposite is true — the battery was already capturing essentially all the curtailment events a 2013-era system could reach, and the real limit was the shape/timing of curtailment events rather than battery size — a bigger battery would simply sit idle more often, and the real cost-per-MWh-avoided would be worse than this naive figure suggests. It also excludes O&M, financing, and replacement costs over the asset's life (though LFP's 5–8x longer cycle life should reduce replacement need relative to lead-acid), and prices only the battery, not the whole NINES project or the ANM system separately. Nobody has published the real system-design study that would resolve this. What the calculation does establish credibly is direction and rough scale, not a validated answer.

**Why this reframes rather than resolves the argument.** The ANM system's own stated curtailment benefit (1.2GWh/year, per the 4B report) was already roughly 11x larger than the domestic DSM layer's 105.7 MWh/year — on 2013-era hardware, before any of the above cost reduction is even applied. So "ANM + modern LFP storage, minus domestic DSM" is not simply dropping the hard part of NINES; it's scaling up the part that was already doing most of the useful work, at a fraction of the historical cost. That is a genuinely different-looking proposition than the one NINES actually tested — but it remains a hypothetical rebuild, not a costed comparison anyone has run, and it does not change the historical finding that no demand-side option was in the 2019 appraisal.

*Caveat:* these are illustrative, order-of-magnitude figures from global/GB-wide battery market surveys (BloombergNEF, NREL, Ember, EIA) and general LFP-vs-lead-acid technical comparisons, not a costed re-engineering of the NINES scheme itself — a genuine "NINES redone in 2026" figure would need a proper system design (battery sizing, balance-of-plant, installation costs specific to Shetland) that hasn't been attempted here.

## 8. Present-day parallels

Section 7 asked a counterfactual — what NINES-era technology would cost and do if bought today. This section is different in kind: it isn't hypothetical, it's two live, ongoing cases showing this project's underlying question is still active right now, one at the network-planning level and one at the deployed-product level.

**8.1 Gremista GSP (Shetland) Strategic Development Plan — the same question, the same island, a decade on.** SSEN's Gremista GSP Strategic Development Plan is a published-for-consultation planning document, not a formal multi-hundred-million-pound options appraisal on the scale of the 2019 Baringa report — it sits earlier and lighter in SSEN's planning process, closer to a distribution-level needs assessment than a sanctioned investment decision. With that scale difference noted, the pattern it shows is the same one found in section 5. It identifies secondary transformers "projected to be overloaded by 2028 (under the Consumer Transformation scenario)" concentrated in the northern part of the supply area, explicitly notes that "more vulnerable groups may have lower level of adoption of LCTs and therefore provide less ability to manage overloads through flexibility services" (using SSEN's own Vulnerability Future Energy Scenarios groupings), and states that "flexibility at a local level... is likely to be required alongside traditional reinforcement" — but, as with the 2019 interconnector appraisal, does not cost that flexibility option as a genuine alternative to reinforcement, only as a supporting measure alongside it. This extends the note's core finding into the present day, and, notably, is on the very same island as the original case — the same operator appears to be repeating the same framing a decade later.

**8.2 Kight PowerHub (Dumfries, South of Scotland) — the negawatt side, already being manufactured.** Kight PowerHub (founder Lawrence Fagg) announced in March 2026 that it is opening Scotland's first domestic battery-storage factory, in Dumfries, following six years of R&D including four years working directly with South of Scotland Enterprise (SOSE) — with the product itself designed using feedback from South of Scotland Registered Social Landlords. The factory is expected to create up to 700 direct jobs and around 2,000 more across the supply chain.

The product is a 10kWh, AI-enabled domestic battery. One outlet (Saurenergy) describes its chemistry as "NGA" (non-graphite anode), cobalt- and graphite-free — this specific chemistry label was not independently corroborated in a second source and should be treated as single-sourced pending confirmation from Kight's own technical documentation. More consistently reported across coverage: a claimed 20,000-cycle lifespan, a 25-year warranty (described as the longest currently available in domestic storage), and a "zero fire risk" safety claim said to be backed by insurer endorsement.

Most relevant of all: this isn't a lab prototype. Units were already installed in South of Scotland social housing during 2025, with Kight claiming an average 80% reduction in tenant electricity bills — a figure that is the company's own claim, not independently audited in any source found, and should be quoted with that caveat attached every time. But even with that caveat, the underlying fact stands: a Scottish company, co-designing its product directly with social housing landlords, is now manufacturing purpose-built domestic storage for exactly the population this project is concerned with — in the region immediately south of Shetland, roughly a decade after NINES ran. It is a stronger illustration than the general LFP/ESS/CATL market comparison in section 7 of how fast and how locally the "negawatt" side of this argument has moved, and is worth citing by name if this note is ever shared with NESO, Ofgem, or a DNO audience with a Scottish social-housing remit.

**Read together:** Gremista shows the megawatt-side pattern (flexibility named, not costed) persisting into the present; Kight shows the negawatt-side option existing, deployed, and getting manufactured at scale, in the same region, at the same time. Section 8.1 and 8.2 are not shown here as having been compared to one another by any operator — that comparison, as far as this note has found, still hasn't happened. That absence is, itself, the point.

## 9. Why this still matters: the same problem, now at national scale

Section 4 established the most likely reason NINES never made it into the 2019 appraisal: a large, revenue-generating megawatt project (Viking Energy Wind Farm's CfD-subsidised export need) arrived with a hard commercial deadline and out-prioritised a diffuse, cost-avoiding negawatt question that had no equivalently concentrated advocate. Put simply: Viking's export represented concentrated, contracted, legally-owed money for a specific company; NINES's demand-side value was spread across the whole system and owed to no one in particular. When those two kinds of value compete for regulatory attention, the concentrated one wins by default, absent a rule that forces an explicit comparison. That is a real, general pattern in UK network economics, not a one-off failing specific to SSEN or Shetland.

What makes this more than a historical postscript is that the underlying problem the interconnector was built to solve — Shetland's wind having nowhere to go — has not actually gone away. It has moved downstream, and it has grown by roughly three orders of magnitude.

**Viking Energy Wind Farm is being curtailed at scale despite the £641.8m interconnector.** Per UKERC (the UK Energy Research Centre, an independent academic body, not a campaigning source), Viking has run at approximately 60% curtailment across its first couple of years of operation — comparable to two other flagship Scottish wind farms, Seagreen (66%) and Moray East (38%). More recent, Viking-specific figures corroborate this at scale: SSE received £2,137,687 in constraint payments for Viking in August 2024 alone; for full-year 2025, Viking discarded 1,100,738 MWh — 27% of all Scotland's wasted onshore wind energy — earning £9,631,696 in constraint payments; and cumulative constraint payments since Viking became operational in August 2023 have reached roughly £19m (Shetland News, January 2026). This is not because the Shetland-to-mainland link itself is undersized for Viking's output; SSE's own public statement attributes it directly to transmission bottlenecks rather than local capacity — "the UK has built renewables where the resources are strongest, and now we need to upgrade the grid to eliminate bottlenecks so we can harness more of that energy, more of the time" — pointing to the Scotland-England transmission boundary (the B4/B6 boundaries), well south of Shetland, compounded by network unavailability from ongoing maintenance work that UKERC estimates reduced available transfer capacity by roughly 2GW during 2024 alone.

**The cost of this, nationally, is not small.** UKERC's analysis puts GB-wide thermal constraint costs at £1.65 billion over just 15 months (January 2024 to April 2025), with fleet-wide average curtailment running around 11.5% of available wind energy since early 2023. Separately, Shetland News reports £116m in curtailment payments across Great Britain in the first half of 2025 alone (a 15% increase on the same period the year before) — paid for by consumers through their bills, for electricity that was generated and then not used.

**What this is, and is not, a live version of.** It is tempting to frame this as "storage versus transmission" — the same negawatt-vs-megawatt question NINES asked, just at national scale. That framing is too neat, and worth correcting explicitly. Viking's curtailment is caused by a full transmission boundary between Scotland and England, not by a lack of local demand or storage capacity at Viking itself. A battery sited near Viking cannot export power past a boundary that is already at capacity, any more than the wind farm itself can — storage does not add transmission capacity. What a battery *can* do is time-shift Viking's generation: absorb output during the hours the boundary is congested, and either discharge into local demand or export later when the boundary has headroom. That is a genuinely different, and technically harder, value proposition than "soak up local curtailment," and it is the honest version of what NINES's underlying question looks like at this scale. As far as this note has found, nobody has published a systematic, costed answer to *that* specific question — whether storage sited near curtailed wind farms could reduce constraint payments by time-shifting generation into uncongested periods, and if so, at what scale and cost — applied to Viking or to the wider Scotland-England constraint problem. That is the live, answerable question this history points to; "can storage substitute for transmission" is not, because storage cannot solve a full-boundary problem the way more transmission capacity can.

*Caveat:* Viking's capacity is reported inconsistently across sources checked in this note — 457MW (2019 Ofgem filing), 443MW (SSE Renewables, 2022), 433MW (UKERC), and 370MW (a 2024 retrospective) — a discrepancy this note has not been able to fully resolve, likely reflecting design changes and different reporting conventions (consented capacity vs. contracted CfD capacity vs. installed capacity) rather than any single figure being wrong. Use a rounded "in the low-to-mid 400s MW" rather than quoting any one figure with false precision. The £1.65bn and £116m figures are GB-wide totals across many constrained generators, not Viking-specific costs, and should be presented as system-scale context for the problem, not as Viking's own cost; the £2.14m (Aug 2024), £9.63m (2025), and £19m (cumulative since Aug 2023) figures, by contrast, are Viking-specific and sourced directly to Shetland News reporting on SSE's own disclosed constraint-payment data. Curtailment percentages for Viking also vary by period and source (roughly 57–78% depending on the specific month and source cited) — this note has not attempted to reconcile them into one figure, and none should be quoted as a stable annual rate without checking the specific period it describes.

## 10. A dimension NINES's own evaluation never measured: fuel poverty

Everything in sections 3–9 measures NINES and the interconnector on the terms their own reports used: curtailment avoided (£/MWh), capacity delivered (£/MW), renewable generation connected (MW), cost (£). None of the NINES evaluation reports checked for this note — economics, ANM operational, or battery operational — measure the outcome that arguably matters most for a trial that put smart heating controls into 234 (later 223) homes on one of the most fuel-poor parts of the UK: whether those households ended up any warmer, or any cheaper to heat.

**Shetland's fuel poverty rate is high, and confirmed as such by the Scottish Government's own data.** The Scottish House Condition Survey Local Authority Tables 2022–2024 (published 26 May 2026) put Shetland Islands' fuel poverty rate at 40%, one of eight local authorities with a rate "statistically significantly higher than the national average" of 31% — behind only Orkney Islands (51%) among all Scottish local authorities, and level with Na h-Eileanan Siar, also at 40%. Separately, the Shetland Times (26 October 2025) reported that 1,854 people in Shetland, including 614 children, received emergency fuel vouchers from the Fuel Bank Foundation between January 2023 and the article's publication — a figure corroborated by the Fuel Bank Foundation's own account of the same visit.

**Shetland's current local energy strategy treats this as the top priority, without reference to NINES.** SSEN's Local Heat and Energy Efficiency Strategy (LHEES) for Shetland (February 2025) lists "Priority A: Alleviating Fuel Poverty" as its first and most urgent priority, and separately acknowledges that comprehensive fuel poverty data for Shetland is hard to come by ("there is no one data source that provides a definitive measure of fuel poverty in Shetland"). NINES is not mentioned anywhere in this document, despite NINES having put smart heating control into several hundred Shetland homes less than a decade earlier — a trial that, whatever its curtailment-economics findings, would seem directly relevant to a strategy document about heating vulnerable Shetland households, yet left no visible trace in it.

**What this does and doesn't show.** It doesn't show that NINES made fuel poverty worse, or that it should have been designed as a fuel poverty intervention — its stated purpose was grid capacity and curtailment, not household welfare, and it should be judged fairly against the purpose it was actually given. What it does show is a gap in how the trial was evaluated: a project that physically altered the heating systems of several hundred households in one of the most fuel-poor parts of the UK produced economics, ANM, and battery reports, but, as far as this note has found, no report measuring tenant bills, thermal comfort, or fuel poverty status before and after. If a NINES-style trial were designed today, in a place with a documented 40% fuel poverty rate and a strategy document naming that as priority one, it's reasonable to ask why that outcome wouldn't be measured alongside curtailment and capacity — not as a substitute for the grid-side question this note has focused on, but as a second, equally real one the original trial was silent on.

*Caveat:* Shetland's extreme fuel poverty rate (a stricter, sub-category measure) is referenced in some secondary sources as being unusually high locally, but this note could not independently confirm a specific percentage for Shetland from the Scottish Government's own published tables — only the headline 40%/31% figures above are confirmed to source. Any more specific extreme-fuel-poverty percentage for Shetland should be treated as unconfirmed until traced to the full Local Authority Tables dataset rather than a summary press release. Similarly, a claimed direct quote from a Fuel Bank Foundation representative describing conditions in Shetland (reported in some secondary coverage) could not be verified verbatim in the sources checked and should not be quoted directly without a primary citation.

## 11. What would strengthen this further

**Update (2026-09-13): NINES operational and economics reports checked directly.** Three further Strathclyde/Strathprints NINES evaluation reports were reviewed — 4B (ANM Operational Effectiveness), 2A (Battery Operational Effectiveness), and the 6A economics report referenced earlier — plus an attempt to reach the 7A (Knowledge and Learning) report and the original ninessmartgrid.co.uk site.

*The capacity discrepancy is now better explained, though not fully resolved.* The 4B ANM report gives a concrete figure: **8.545MW of ANM-controlled renewable generation (ACG)** was connected under NINES (North Hoo 0.5MW, Luggie's Knowe 3MW, Shetland Tidal Array 0.5MW, Garth Wind Farm 4.25MW), against a **14.3MW frequency-stability limit** the system was designed to accommodate during loss-of-generation events. That 8.5–14.3MW range sits almost exactly on top of the "between 8 and 14 MW" estimate found earlier in the 2014 Ofgem workshop presentation — so the "8-14MW" figure most likely refers to this ANM-connected-generation capacity, not the separate 7MW figure SSEN's policy-history document uses for the demand/storage-side target against the replacement power station. In other words, there appear to be two different, correctly-sourced capacity figures for two different parts of NINES (generation-side ANM vs. demand/storage-side capacity substitution), not one contradiction — but this is not confirmed anywhere in the source documents explicitly cross-referencing the two, so it remains an inference, not a documented reconciliation.

**Still no genuine cost-per-kW-of-capacity-deferred figure exists in any NINES report checked** (economics, ANM operational, or battery operational). The reports consistently frame cost-effectiveness in £/MWh of curtailment avoided, not £/kW or £/MW of capacity: the battery's CAPEX was £3,974,000 (close to, though not identical to, the £4.12m figure in the economics report — likely different accounting boundaries), against a curtailment-mitigation cost of £203.14/MWh (falling to a projected £157.39/MWh at higher fuel prices), and the battery underperformed its own utilisation target (roughly 70% of the expected 300 cycles/0.9GWh per year, worse under automated ANM scheduling than manual). The ANM system's separately-stated benefit is a projected 1.2GWh/year reduction in curtailment. None of this converts cleanly into a £/kW-avoided figure comparable to the interconnector's £/MW cost — the conclusion from the original write-up holds and is now more firmly evidenced: **this comparison was never made in the primary NINES documentation, not because it was overlooked here, but because NINES was evaluated throughout on an energy (£/MWh) basis, while the interconnector was evaluated on a capacity (£/MW) basis. The two evaluation frameworks are simply incommensurate as published.**

- The 7A Knowledge and Learning report (Bell et al., 2017) could not be retrieved (403 error on the PurePortal-hosted PDF); its abstract describes it as covering research/learning outcomes rather than capacity or cost figures, so it is unlikely to change this conclusion, but it hasn't been ruled out.

**Update (2026-09-13): primary-source talk by SSEN's Head of Innovation.** A 2017 IET.tv conference talk by Stewart Reid corroborates and sharpens several figures independently of the written reports. It confirms 8.5MW of renewable generation connected to the Shetland network by project end (matching the ANM report's 8.545MW almost exactly), gives a genuine concrete demand-side capacity figure not found elsewhere — **234 homes fitted with smart storage heaters/hot water control delivering 1.5MW of frequency response** — and states the battery (1MW/3MWh) was originally specified as sodium-sulphur but switched to lead-acid (VRLA) before installation over safety concerns, correcting the chemistry given in earlier drafts of this note (now fixed in section 3 above). The talk is also candid about scale: Reid himself notes 1.5MW of demand flexibility and a 1MW battery are "not a big proportion" of total island demand, framing NINES explicitly as a proof-of-concept for what a fully-enabled island could achieve rather than a capacity solution sized to fully answer Shetland's needs — which is consistent with, and reinforces, this note's core finding that NINES and the interconnector were never evaluated as substitutes for one another. The talk does not mention cost-per-MW/kW figures or any comparison with the interconnector; it is a technical/operational retrospective, not an economics discussion.
- `ninessmartgrid.co.uk` is confirmed genuinely offline (DNS resolution failure, not a tool-side restriction) — this affects direct access but not the conclusion, since the same content is mirrored on Strathprints and ssen-innovation.co.uk. Andy checked archive.org directly and found nothing there either — the site is not preserved in the Wayback Machine in a usable form. - Shetland's specific extreme fuel poverty rate (section 10) — the headline 40%/31% fuel poverty figures are confirmed to the Scottish Government's Local Authority Tables press release, but the stricter extreme-fuel-poverty percentage for Shetland specifically was not locatable in the sources checked and would need the full dataset rather than the summary release.
