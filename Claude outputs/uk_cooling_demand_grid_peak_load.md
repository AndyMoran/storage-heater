# UK Cooling Demand and Grid Peak Load: Ground Truth

**Prepared by:** Andy Moran, Heaviside Analytics
**Date:** September 2026
**Status:** Phase 1 of 2, frozen after red-team review (September 2026) — this establishes the ground truth on cooling demand and grid peak-load risk, and deliberately stops here rather than chasing further Phase-1 leads (EHS raw data, BSRIA sales figures, a primary NESO statement on the June 2026 event), which would carry diminishing returns against an already-defensible evidence boundary. Phase 2 (not yet started) will compare cooling-delivery technologies — air conditioning, reversible air-to-air heat pumps, air-to-water heat pumps, and Peltier/thermoelectric reversible heat pumps (including TCS's Hummingbird) — against that ground truth, starting from the source register in Section 10. Updated once since freezing, to add mortality context (Section 6) following further research.

Every figure below is tagged: **confirmed directly** (read from the primary source itself), **triangulated** (consistent secondary reporting, primary not independently reached), or **not verified** (no credible source found). ## 1. Is the UK grid still winter-peaking, or is a summer peak emerging?

**Still clearly winter-peaking. No primary source claims an imminent shift to a summer/cooling-driven peak.**

GB's annual peak demand in 2025 was 49.1 GW, recorded 9 January 2025 at 17:00 — a winter evening. Minimum demand that year was 16.6 GW (24 August 2025, 04:00). Typical winter evening peaks run around 28.8 GW against a summer evening maximum of roughly 20.7 GW — **triangulated**, via Electricitymaps' "Grid in Review 2025: Great Britain," an independent aggregator working from National Grid transmission data rather than a NESO document itself.

An older DECC analysis (*Seasonal variations in electricity demand*, March 2014) — **confirmed directly** — compared a winter peak day (12 December 2012) against a summer peak day (23 July 2013) and found winter demand 36% higher, though the data is now over a decade old and the document gives no absolute GW figures.

Even during 2026's exceptional heat, demand stayed well below the winter peak: GB demand reached roughly 40 GW around midday on 26 June 2026, the hottest day of the year — **triangulated**, via Carbon Brief's reporting of the event — still around 9 GW short of the 2025 winter peak.

Most tellingly: NESO's own *Future Energy Scenarios: Pathway Assumptions 2024* — **confirmed directly**, read in full — treats air conditioning as "a potential new growth of electricity demand" rather than a peak-shifting phenomenon, and explicitly assumes **fixed ambient temperatures throughout its modelling period, with no climate-change temperature increase incorporated**. A peer-reviewed May 2026 paper (Simpson et al., see Section 5) states plainly that "official scenario analysis does not yet examine peak loads in summer," and that AC-driven peak demand isn't even presented in the FES 2024 workbooks — **confirmed directly**, read in full.

**Reading this honestly:** there is no evidence in the sources examined that the GB summer peak is close to overtaking the winter peak — that is different from claiming it cannot happen on some longer horizon, which this evidence base doesn't establish either way. What is established more precisely: the FES 2024 demand assumptions do not dynamically incorporate future warming into ambient-temperature assumptions — a methodological observation about NESO's current modelling scope, not a claim that NESO ignores climate change or is unaware of the issue.

## 2. UK domestic air conditioning penetration vs. other countries

Penetration estimates for the UK are unusually inconsistent across sources, and that inconsistency is itself a finding worth stating rather than smoothing over. The more credible official-survey-derived figures put UK domestic AC penetration in the region of 3–5%: 2.8% (2011), 2.3% (2017), and 3.1% (2021/22 survey year) per official-survey citations in a May 2026 peer-reviewed paper (Simpson et al., UCL) — **confirmed as reported in that paper**, though the underlying English Housing Survey table was not independently pulled in this pass. The same paper flags two more recent, much higher figures (8% from a 2024 opt-in survey, 19% from a 2023 "representative" survey) explicitly as an example of poor data quality in this space, not as reliable estimates — they are reproduced here only to show the range in circulation, not as usable numbers. A University of Reading estimate puts England at roughly 4% (2026), and the Financial Times cites under 5% for the UK overall — both **triangulated** via Carbon Brief.

For international comparison (all **triangulated** via Carbon Brief's July/August 2026 "Eight facts about air conditioning" piece, which cites national statistical bodies it cannot be independently checked against in this pass): Germany ~6% (2020, Umweltbundesamt), France ~25% (2020, ADEME), Spain ~41% (2024, Idealista), Italy ~60% (2024, Istat), Greece 70–80% (2025, Greenpeace), EU average ~20% (2026, IEA), US >90% (2026, US Census Bureau).

**Reading this honestly:** on the more credible figures, the UK sits at the very low end of European AC penetration, well behind France and dramatically behind southern Europe and the US.

## 3. Is UK cooling demand actually growing, and why?

The climate-driver side of this question is well-evidenced and **confirmed directly** from the Met Office (*UK and Global extreme events – Heatwaves*, and the August 2026 blog *UK heatwaves: how the records tell a changing story*, both read in full):

- Average warm-spell duration has more than doubled: 5.3 days (1961–90 baseline) to over 13 days (2008–2017) nationally; in South East England specifically, from ~6 days to over 18 days/year across the same periods.
- "Tropical nights" (minimum temperature not falling below 20°C) have occurred in all but three years so far this century, versus only half of years between 1961 and 2000.
- The Met Office states directly that "the increase in the frequency, duration, and intensity of these events over recent decades is clearly linked to the observed warming of the planet and can be attributed to human activity."
- By mid-century, a summer as warm as 2018 is projected to have roughly a 50% chance of occurring, versus under 10% in 1981–2000.
- The UK's first-ever recorded temperature above 40°C came on 19 July 2022 (40.3°C, Coningsby, Lincolnshire) — 1.6°C above the prior record. The 2026 summer's June–August heatwave period (30 June – 5 August) was the second-longest on record at 37 days, behind only 1995's 39 days.

The behavioural/uptake side of the question is much weaker: no credible, dated time series for UK domestic AC unit sales could be found in this pass. BSRIA's actual sales data sits behind a membership paywall, and the "market size" figures circulating on commercial market-research sites carry unverifiable headline growth-rate claims with no visible primary data behind them — these are explicitly **not verified** and are excluded rather than cited. This is a genuine gap, not a settled "no" — a next step would be BSRIA membership access or data from the Heating and Ventilating Contractors' Association / Eurovent.

**Reading this honestly:** the case that the UK is getting hotter, more often, is solid and government-confirmed. The case that people are actually buying and running more AC as a result is plausible but not currently demonstrated with verifiable data.

## 4. Historical evidence of heatwaves stressing the UK grid

**The widely-repeated "coal plant fired up" story (2023) is real but more nuanced than usually reported, and could not be traced to a National Grid ESO primary statement in this pass.** Ratcliffe-on-Soar coal power station was brought back online around 12–13 June 2023, ending a 46-day coal-free run on the GB grid — **triangulated** across multiple press outlets (ITV News, PowerMag), none of which quote National Grid ESO directly; the original Guardian report and any ESO market notice were both inaccessible in this pass. Reported contributing factors were multiple and simultaneous: temperatures crossing 30°C for the first time that year, low wind (~7% of the generation mix that day), planned maintenance reducing Torness nuclear station's output by 1.3 GW, and a fault on the 1.4 GW North Sea Link interconnector. No source gave a specific GW figure for AC-driven demand itself — this was a multi-factor system event, not a clean "air conditioning broke the grid" story, and should be presented that way.

**More recent, more clearly documented: 24–26 June 2026.** On 24 June 2026, NESO paid up to £1,400/MWh to secure roughly 1.7 GW of imported power — reported as nearly 20 times the average June 2025 electricity price — with NESO's stated cause being extremely high temperatures across GB and the continent combined with low wind. On 26 June, GB demand held around 40 GW at midday (unusually high and flat for a summer day), solar peaked at 13.9 GW, and wind fell to roughly 15% of the generation mix against a ~30% average. Both figures are **triangulated** via Carbon Brief's 4 August 2026 reporting, which appears to draw on NESO's own market data, though the underlying NESO notice was not fetched directly in this pass.

**Reading this honestly:** the June 2026 event provides a concrete, expensive example of summer system stress under a combination of high temperatures, elevated demand, and low wind — arguably a stronger and more current example than the frequently-cited 2023 coal episode — but it does not isolate cooling demand as the cause, and shouldn't be read as evidence of cooling-induced electricity stress specifically. Its real value for this project is different: it establishes the system conditions (heat coinciding with low renewable output) into which any cooling technology will be introduced, which is exactly what Phase 2 needs as background, independent of how much of that demand is AC-driven.

## 5. Quantified UK projections for cooling-driven peak demand

This is the strongest, most directly-sourced material in this document — two documents, both read in full.

**UKERC, *Domestic Air Conditioning in 2050*** (Crawley, Ogunrin, Taneja, Vorushylo, Wang; 20 October 2020) — **confirmed directly**. Using socio-technical uptake scenarios combined with dynamic half-hourly building simulation, integrated into National Grid's "Two Degrees" 2050 Future Energy Scenario, the central finding is that 5–32% of English households adopt AC by 2050 depending on scenario — notably lower than some earlier, simpler estimates. In the worst case modelled (32% uptake), summer peak load rises by 7 GW, concentrated in the evening. Critically, the authors themselves note that the winter evening peak from heat-pump/heating electrification could exceed this projected summer AC peak by up to a factor of two — even in their worst-case AC scenario, winter heating electrification remains the larger peak-demand driver.

**Simpson, Halai, Price, Petrou, Heaviside, Davies, *Air conditioning and the future electricity system in Great Britain***, *Energy Research & Social Science* vol. 136, 5 May 2026 (UCL Bartlett School) — **confirmed directly**, full PDF read via UCL Discovery. This paper compiles a wide range of competing estimates: a BEIS baseline of 11.3 GW (2020) rising to 11.9 GW (2030) and up to 19.0 GW under a high-warming (4°C) scenario by 2100; a 2017 FES "Consumer Power" scenario implying 39 GW at two-thirds household penetration; and first-principles per-household calculations spanning 18–44 GW depending on assumptions. Current FES 2024 pathways assume 1% AC penetration in 2023, rising to 10–18 million homes with AC by 2050 under higher-uptake pathways, versus no growth under the net-zero-aligned "Holistic Transition" pathway — this matches figures read directly from the FES 2024 primary document itself (10 million properties by 2050, +5 TWh/year additional demand, 191 running hours/year, 2.7 kW average unit rating). The paper's most important finding for this analysis: peak demand from AC is not presented in NESO's own FES 2024 workbooks at all, and official scenario analysis does not yet examine summer peak loads or their interaction with low-wind, low-solar afternoon periods — precisely the gap the June 2026 heatwave incident (Section 4) exposed in practice.

**Reading this honestly:** published estimates of potential cooling-related peak demand span roughly 7 GW to the high-teens or high-30s GW, depending heavily on assumptions and methodology — different years, penetration assumptions, climate scenarios, definitions of peak, and possibly different appliance assumptions and geographies. Simpson et al.'s own contribution is precisely to demonstrate how heterogeneous these estimates are; that heterogeneity means this is a wide spread of published figures, not a credible probabilistic uncertainty interval, and it should not be interpreted as one. What does hold across every source in this spread: winter heating electrification is a larger peak-demand driver than summer cooling, now and in every scenario modelled to 2050.

## 6. Mortality: putting the cooling question into perspective

This section adds context, not a new demand estimate — it doesn't change any GW or kWh figure elsewhere in this document, but it usefully separates "heat is dangerous" from "heat is already the UK's dominant temperature-related killer," which it is not.

**The headline UK picture, confirmed directly** from ONS's *Climate-related mortality, England and Wales: 1988 to 2025* bulletin (read in full): over that 37-year period, England recorded 40,063 deaths associated with the hottest days against 138,944 associated with the coldest days — cold-associated mortality roughly 3.5 times higher. Wales recorded 2,070 hot-day deaths against 10,098 cold-day deaths — roughly 4.9 times higher. ONS states directly that "the proportion of deaths associated with the hottest days has increased in recent years, and the gap between heat- and cold-related deaths is narrowing." For 2022 specifically — an exceptional heat year, coinciding with the UK's first-ever recorded temperature above 40°C — the same bulletin gives an estimated 4,430 heat-related deaths in England and 222 in Wales. (One flagged discrepancy: earlier reporting cited a figure of 4,507 for this same 2022 England estimate; this document uses 4,430 as read directly from the current ONS bulletin, which may reflect a methodology revision between releases — worth a quick check against the specific 2022-year ONS release if the precise figure matters for future citation.)

**At the individual-episode level, cold remains extremely consequential — confirmed directly** from two UKHSA reports read directly: winter 2024–25 recorded 2,544 cold-associated deaths in England across three cold episodes between November 2024 and January 2025 (the most severe, a 6-day period in early January, alone accounted for 1,630 deaths); summer 2025 recorded 1,504 heat-associated deaths across five heat episodes — itself substantially lower than UKHSA's own modelled prediction of 3,039 for that summer. **An important methodological caveat, confirmed directly from UKHSA's own reporting:** these two figures should not simply be divided to declare cold "more dangerous," because UKHSA uses different methodologies for cold and heat mortality specifically because cold mortality is lagged rather than acute — mortality "peaks around 5 days after cold weather and remains elevated for up to 9 days," with heart-related deaths increasing first and respiratory/infection-related deaths showing longer delays still. Heat mortality, by contrast, tracks much more closely with the heat period itself — directly relevant to this project's grid-timing interest, since acute heat mortality risk aligns far more closely with the electricity-demand event (afternoon/evening AC load coinciding with heat) than cold mortality's multi-day lagged profile does with a cold snap's own demand peak.

**The European picture shows heat mortality can be genuinely enormous, and that adaptation matters more than raw temperature.** A Nature Medicine study estimated 61,672 heat-related deaths across Europe during summer 2022 (30 May – 4 September), with Italy (18,010), Spain (11,324), and Germany (8,173) the highest absolute totals — **confirmed directly**, read from the study itself. A follow-up by the same research group estimated 47,690 heat-related deaths across 35 European countries in 2023 — **confirmed directly** — and found that mortality "would have been +80.0% higher in absence of present-century adaptation," rising to +100.7% higher among people aged 80+. That adaptation finding is the important one for this project: heat mortality is not simply a function of how hot a country gets, but of how well-adapted its population, housing, and health systems are to the heat it experiences.

**Finland is a genuinely useful counterexample for the adaptation point, though one specific claim about it should not be used.** A BMJ Open register-based study of Finland covering 1971–2019 found that for 2000–2019, winter mortality was only about 11% higher than non-winter mortality for men and 14% higher for women — **confirmed directly**, read from the study's own abstract — a remarkably small excess given Finland's severe winter climate, and one the authors describe as having declined substantially over the five-decade study period. The implication worth drawing explicitly: cold exposure is not the same thing as cold mortality — insulated housing, reliable heating, healthcare access, preparedness, and public-health warning systems appear to mediate the relationship heavily. **One claim from this same research area should not go into this document yet:** a comparison of winter mortality specifically among homeless people in Finland versus other countries was not found in this pass and should be treated as unverified rather than cited, even though Finland's low general excess-winter-mortality finding and evidence of elevated overall mortality among homeless populations are each independently well-supported.

**Predictability is a further asymmetry, now confirmed directly.** Lavaysse, Naumann, Alfieri, Salamon & Vogt, *Predictability of the European heat and cold waves*, *Climate Dynamics* 52, 2481–2495 (2019), an ECMWF ensemble hindcast study — **confirmed directly**, read from the paper itself — finds explicitly that "the scores show a higher predictive skill for the cold waves (in winter) than for the heat waves (in summer)," with forecasts skillful up to 15 days for heat waves and up to 21 days for cold waves. The authors are more cautious about the operationally useful range, however: skill "strongly decreases during the first week of forecasts (from 80 to 40% of observed events correctly forecasted)," and they state that "forecasts at 1-week lead time stay robust" while "the limit of predictability is completely lost beyond 2-weeks lead time." The safer formulation, and the one used here: European cold-wave events have been found to exhibit higher forecast skill than heat-wave events, with useful predictive skill extending further into the forecast horizon, in this ECMWF ensemble hindcast study — not the broader claim that cold spells are inherently easier to predict than heat spells in general, since the paper itself shows predictability depends on lead time, spatial scale, event definition, and location (large-scale events are more predictable than localised ones). The UK's UKHSA/Met Office Cold-Health Alert system, which operates forecast tiers at 0–5, 6–15, and 16–30 days, is consistent with this finding but doesn't independently prove it.

This sharpens the Finland comparison rather than simplifying it: predictability and adaptation/preparedness are separate mechanisms, and Finland's low winter mortality may benefit from both a well-adapted population and infrastructure *and* relatively predictable winter conditions — a more nuanced picture than "cold is just less dangerous in Finland," and one worth keeping distinct in any future framing of this material.

**Reading this honestly, and why it matters for Phase 2:** temperature-related mortality in Britain is not symmetric — cold has historically caused substantially more deaths than heat, and remains the larger burden at the individual-episode level even in a recent below-average heat summer (2,544 cold deaths in winter 2024–25 versus 1,504 heat deaths in summer 2025) — but the gap is narrowing as heat-related mortality rises, and the European experience shows that gap can close dramatically, and quickly, where adaptation lags behind a changing climate. Finland demonstrates the reverse is also true: severe cold does not translate into proportionately high mortality where housing, heating, and preparedness are adapted to it. The implication for this project's central question is that future UK cooling demand should not be inferred from temperature alone — adoption and adaptation are the mediating variables, which is precisely why Section 3's weak behavioural/uptake evidence matters as much as Section 3's strong climate evidence. Framed as a single question for the electricity system: not simply how often extreme heat will occur, but how much cooling adoption and behavioural adaptation occurs before the frequency and severity of extreme heat outpaces the protection current adaptation provides. That is very close to the actual question Phase 2 needs to answer, and it sharpens rather than duplicates the causal-chain framing in Section 7.

## 7. Cooling-delivery categories — factual scoping only

This section deliberately stops short of a comparative analysis; that is Phase 2's job. One framing point worth fixing now, though: air conditioning and air-to-air heat pumps are not competing technologies in the way "AC vs heat pump" implies — a reversible split AC unit is, technically, an air-to-air heat pump. The distinction that will actually matter for Phase 2 is one of system architecture and incremental grid load, not category labels: existing heating stock with separately-added cooling, versus a reversible integrated heating/cooling system, versus a hydronic (air-to-water) heat pump with cooling capability added on, versus a thermoelectric system. Framed this way, four categories exist and their basic cooling capability is established as follows:

- **Standalone/split air conditioning units** provide cooling as their primary or sole function by design; some reversible split systems also heat. Not in dispute.
- **Air-to-air heat pumps** are, by definition, reversible split systems — DESNZ's own literature review states they are "electric systems capable of both heating and cooling," with "reversible" meaning the refrigerant cycle runs one direction for heating and reverses for cooling. **Confirmed directly** from DESNZ/Energy Innovation Research Office, *Air-to-Air Heat Pumps* literature review (March 2025, Parkinson, Khalili, Holland, Bowick). The same document confirms air-to-water systems dominate the UK domestic heat pump market, that roughly 95% of reversible air-to-air installations in the UK are in non-domestic settings, and states outright that "little data is available on energy demand for domestic cooling in the UK" — an official government acknowledgment of the same evidence gap this document has run into repeatedly.
- **Air-to-water heat pumps**, confirmed as the UK's dominant installed type by the same document, distribute heat via water-based emitters (radiators, underfloor heating, fan coils) and typically do not provide cooling as standard in UK domestic installations, though some can be configured for it via fan coil units or cooling-mode underfloor heating where specifically designed in. No single primary source was found stating this as a direct quote — this is a reasonable inference from the literature review's framing, not a confirmed fact, and is flagged for a firmer citation (HPA or MCS) in Phase 2. That caution is deliberate and should be preserved into Phase 2 rather than tidied into a confident statement.
- **Peltier/thermoelectric reversible heat pumps** (e.g., TCS's Hummingbird) are noted here only as a fourth category — their heating-and-cooling capability was already established directly from TCS's own product page in the companion TCS analysis and is not re-verified here. Keeping this quarantine (not re-analysing TCS's claims inside Phase 1's evidence base) matters: it avoids the trap of working backwards from an interesting technology to a conclusion, rather than starting from the underlying need.

**A note on the right metric for Phase 2.** AC penetration (Section 2) is a weak proxy for the thing that actually matters. A household's cooling need can be met by portable or split AC, heat-pump cooling, fans, passive design, shading, ventilation, or behavioural adaptation — and an installed unit says nothing about how often it runs, at what setpoint, or whether its use coincides with system stress. The metric Phase 2 should actually be chasing is closer to *GW of coincident electrical cooling load during relevant system-stress windows* (the kind of afternoon/evening, low-wind, high-temperature conditions documented in Section 4) — not percentage of homes owning a unit. This is also where the July 2026 DESNZ report noted below becomes directly relevant, since it models exactly this kind of domestic performance and suitability question.

## 8. What this means, held together

The evidence in this document separates cleanly into a causal chain — climate change → rising overheating/cooling requirement → technology adoption → electricity demand → coincident system peak — and the strength of the evidence drops sharply at each link. The first link (climate) is well-evidenced and government-confirmed. The second (whether people are actually adopting more cooling technology as a result) has only emerging, inconsistent evidence. The third (how much electricity demand that adds) is weakly evidenced, with a wide, non-probabilistic spread of published estimates. The fourth (whether and how that demand coincides with system peak) is barely modelled at all in the UK's own official scenario work. The honest headline follows directly from that: **the UK cooling requirement is a real and growing climate-driven risk, while the resulting electricity demand remains poorly quantified and under-modelled** — not "cooling demand is a real, growing risk" as a single claim, since that conflates links the evidence doesn't support equally.

Put another way, this document's research question has shifted over the course of the work from "how much will cooling increase UK electricity demand" to something more defensible and, on reflection, more interesting: **the UK does not currently have sufficiently good public evidence to quantify how an increasing cooling requirement translates into coincident electricity-system peak demand.** Multiple independent strands point at the same conclusion — weak AC penetration and sales data, poor behavioural evidence, DESNZ's own literature review stating there is "little data...available on energy demand for domestic cooling in the UK," FES 2024 not exposing summer AC peak demand at all, and a wide divergence across every published estimate that does exist. That's a strong foundation precisely because it identifies where the evidence breaks down, rather than pretending it's better than it is.

What is solidly established, and survives scrutiny: the UK is still decisively winter-peaking; climate-driven cooling requirements are increasing; summer heat can already coincide with difficult system conditions (low wind, high demand, expensive emergency balancing); but the electricity-system consequences of domestic cooling remain poorly quantified, and current official modelling does not adequately expose that summer peak-risk question. That is a defensible platform for Phase 2's technology comparison, and importantly it doesn't prejudge any particular technology, including TCS's Hummingbird.

The genuinely open questions for Phase 2, reframed around system architecture rather than category labels (see Section 7): whether adding separate cooling equipment to the existing stock of heating systems, an integrated reversible system, a hydronic heat pump with cooling capability added on, or a thermoelectric system is best positioned to meet a growing but currently under-quantified cooling requirement without making the underlying problem (afternoon/evening peak coincidence with low wind and solar) worse. The right metric to evaluate that against is coincident electrical cooling load during system-stress windows (Section 7), not headline adoption figures. Section 6's mortality evidence adds one further dimension to that: adoption and adaptation, not temperature alone, determine how much of the climate-driven cooling requirement actually converts into electrical demand — the same distinction that separates a poorly-adapted European heat death toll from Finland's well-adapted winter mortality record.
