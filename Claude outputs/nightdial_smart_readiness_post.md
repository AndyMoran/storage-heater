Storage heaters are now named as counting toward EPC Band C.

The January 2026 Home Energy Model consultation lists storage heaters under the technologies that qualify for the new Smart Readiness metric — one of four headline scores on the reformed EPC launching next month. That's a real document, not a rumour. I've read it.

Here's why that matters. From 2030, social landlords must bring every home to EPC Band C. They get to choose the route: fix the fabric, fix the heating system, or prove the home is "smart ready." Each route has its own £10,000 cap. Fabric and heat pumps are both hard — fabric because the real costs vary and aren't fully mapped for this stock, heat pumps because the main grant excludes social housing outright.

Smart Readiness is the cheap way through. And now we know storage heaters count toward it.

I think this might be a get-out-of-jail-free card for landlords, not a fix for tenants. A £100 IoT gadget bolted onto the consumer unit could plausibly pass as "smart heating." The certificate gets its tick. The heater still runs badly. The tenant still can't afford to use it through winter, still reaches for an electric blanket instead. Compliance achieved, nothing that matters actually changed.

There's a second problem, and it's not about tenant comfort — it's the actual point of the metric. A storage heater charged on the wrong night can be worse for the planet than the gas it replaced. Real 2026 figures: heat from gas runs about 203 gCO2 per kWh; heat from the grid averages 177, so storage heating usually beats gas. But UK grid carbon has hit 311 this year, and the coldest, stillest nights — when wind drops and storage heaters do most of their charging — are exactly when it spikes. A gadget that just proves a heater is "connected" doesn't touch that. One that actually shifts charging away from the dirtiest hours would. That's what Smart Readiness is supposed to reward.

I want to be upfront that the exact bar for passing isn't set yet. The consultation closed in March. A fuller government response is due later this year, and it will decide what a device actually has to do to qualify. Nobody knows that number yet — which is exactly why it's worth naming the risk now, before it's locked in.

If storage heaters are going to be the cheap route to compliance either way, the question that matters is what "smart" has to mean. A device that's never been tested against real usage or real carbon timing shouldn't pass the same bar as one that has.
