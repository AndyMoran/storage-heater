**Re:** ED3's "build and flex" framework does not include fabric efficiency as a demand-side option — two independent evidence sources show it should

## Why I'm writing

This letter responds to the RIIO-ED3 Sector Specific Methodology Decision (published May 2026) and its "build and flex" framework for electricity distribution networks. That framework sets out flexibility and other smart alternatives as the intended way to optimise existing network capacity rather than defaulting to reinforcement. It does not include fabric efficiency — insulation-driven permanent demand reduction — as one of those alternatives; the document's own reference to demand reduction is limited to noting that DNOs might take an "enhanced role in the coordination and delivery of measures to reduce energy consumption," without integrating that into the build-and-flex mechanism itself.

I'm writing because two independent pieces of evidence show that gap has a real cost, and because the timing matters: DNOs' RIIO-ED3 business plans are due by the end of 2026, with draft determinations following in 2027 and final determinations by the end of that year. Whatever is or isn't built into "build and flex" now will shape those business plans. This is a case for closing a specific, evidenced gap in a live process, not a general appeal for more attention to energy efficiency.

I'm an independent energy analyst. I came to this question from a different angle originally — a case study of Shetland's NINES demand-management trial, benchmarked against the interconnector later built to serve the island, which asked whether a demand-side alternative was ever weighed against a supply-side one. That case turned out to be inconclusive in an interesting way: a large wind farm's export needs turned out to be the real driver of the capacity decision, so the negawatt-versus-megawatt comparison never really got tested there either way. What follows is more direct, more current, and I think more actionable.

## The industry has already quantified this — and named the barrier itself

Western Power Distribution (now National Grid Electricity Distribution), working with the consultancy Everoze, ran a study — Future Flex — that built efficiency-adjusted half-hourly domestic demand profiles and mapped them directly against known constraint-alleviation costs on GB networks. Their findings, quoting the report directly:

- "In zones with severe and prolonged constraint issues, the installation or improvement of thermal insulation could have a network value of almost £1,000 over its lifetime."
- "Installation of energy efficiency measures in areas with severe network constraints realises approximately seven times more value than in areas with moderate constraints."
- "Energy efficiency delivers five times more value in zones with chronic network constraints (5-year) than in regions with short-term (1-year) constraints."
- "Among homes with electrified heating, the value of energy efficiency varies by heating type, with heat pumps or conventional electric heating delivering three times more value than storage heaters."

This is a GB distribution network operator, using real constraint data, finding that energy efficiency has a genuine, locationally-concentrated network value — not a modelled abstraction, but something they tried to price.

Then the same report names, in its own words, exactly why that value goes uncaptured today. It identifies: dispersed benefits ("the benefits of energy efficiency are dispersed" across parties, so no single party sees enough of the value to act on it); a fragmented funding landscape ("the existing funding landscape is complex," and schemes "fail to join up"); and a structural mismatch, describing network flexibility services as "designed around the DSO" rather than around the home or the household making the efficiency investment — which the report links directly to low consumer engagement.

In short: a DNO went looking for this value, found it, and told the industry it currently has no way to deliver it to the people positioned to capture it.

## The underlying mechanism is independently proven, at national scale

A peer-reviewed study (Guo, Meunier and Saelens, *Energy and Buildings*, June 2024, part of the EU's Horizon 2020 sEEnergies project) modelled the same basic mechanism rigorously, independent of any DNO or UK context. Using representative low-voltage grids in Germany and Italy, with stochastic demand modelling across thousands of simulation repetitions, it quantified how much residential insulation reduces the grid-reinforcement cost triggered by heat pump and rooftop PV uptake. Its central finding, in the authors' own words: comparing the best-insulated scenario against the least-insulated, "the low U-value scenario can reduce the average [grid reinforcement life-cycle cost per dwelling] for the Italian and German grids by up to 19.5% and 30.8%, respectively" — with the German rural grid at 20.2% and the Italian rural grid at 15.5%, and even a moderate improvement in insulation delivering roughly half that reduction in every case studied.

At the scale the authors modelled full heat pump and PV rollout, the aggregate grid-reinforcement cost this represents is not small: up to €1.27 billion for German rural low-voltage networks alone, and up to €6.23 billion for German urban networks — a combined figure the authors themselves total at roughly €7.5 billion for Germany's low-voltage grid alone, before Italy is added.

I want to be precise about what this study does and doesn't show, because the honest version of the claim is the useful one. It is a generic EU methodology applied to representative German and Italian grids, not a GB-specific costed study — the authors say so explicitly, describing it as "an expandable methodology," not a precise national estimate. It also explicitly excludes the cost of the insulation improvement itself from its reinforcement-cost figure — it quantifies the grid-side saving, not a net cost-benefit case for retrofit. And its mechanism (insulation reducing heat pump peak demand) is distinct from, though complementary to, the domestic demand-flexibility mechanism in the Future Flex study above.

What it establishes, robustly and independently of any UK industry source, is that the underlying physical relationship — better-insulated homes materially reduce the grid reinforcement a given level of electrified heating and generation uptake requires — is real, substantial, and not a marginal effect. Combined with Future Flex's GB-specific, DNO-sourced monetisation of that same broad category of value, the case is no longer just plausible; it is quantified from two independent directions.

## What this means, and what I'm asking

Taken together, these two pieces of evidence show a specific, addressable gap, not a case for more research. Future Flex already did the research and named the barrier itself. GB's own network operators can quantify this value; the report that quantifies it says plainly why none of it currently reaches the households or landlords who could deliver it; and an independent academic study confirms the same underlying mechanism holds at national scale elsewhere in Europe. What's missing is a place in the regulatory framework for any of this to land — and "build and flex," as currently defined, doesn't provide one.

I'd ask Ofgem to consider three things, in order of how directly they follow from the evidence and how squarely they sit within Ofgem's own remit:

1. **Require locational negawatt-value assessment in ED3 business plans, using a methodology DNOs already have.** Future Flex has already shown how to do this — building efficiency-adjusted half-hourly demand profiles and mapping them against known constraint-alleviation costs. Where a DNO's business plan includes a reinforcement case driven substantially by domestic electrification (heat pumps, EVs, storage heating), Ofgem could require the options appraisal to include an explicit, costed efficiency alternative using a comparable method — not designed from scratch, since one DNO has already built and published it.

2. **Require DNOs to report where that value is concentrated, so other bodies can act on it.** Future Flex found this value is geographically clustered — concentrated in areas with severe and chronic network constraints. Ofgem could require DNOs to report the location and scale of these high-network-value areas as part of their ED3 business plans. That positions Ofgem as the producer of information DESNZ, local authorities, and retrofit schemes (ECO, GBIS, and others) could use to target funding — a role that fits Ofgem's regulatory remit better than trying to fix the funding fragmentation directly, which spans departments outside Ofgem's control.

3. **Drive a GB-specific version of the Guo/Meunier/Saelens methodology through existing mechanisms**, rather than commissioning fresh academic research directly. The mechanism is proven at EU scale, using representative German and Italian grids; a GB-specific application, using UK grid parameters and heating/insulation stock data, would convert a directionally strong international finding into a number DNOs and Ofgem could use in price control and reinforcement decisions. This could be required as part of DNOs' own locational analysis in ED3 business plans, or driven through the ED3 innovation mechanism (NIA/NIC) rather than treated as a research commission outside Ofgem's normal tools.

I'm not asking Ofgem to take my word for either underlying source — the Future Flex report and its findings are on the public record via commercial.nationalgrid.co.uk, and the academic paper is openly citable at DOI 10.1016/j.enbuild.2024.114436. I'd be glad to share the fuller evidence base behind this letter, including the Shetland case study referenced above, which shows the same underlying pattern — real negawatt value that a formal capacity decision never tested against the megawatt alternative — playing out a decade earlier, in a different form, on a GB network.

I'd welcome the chance to discuss this further.
